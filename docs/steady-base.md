## Why this exists

John, 30 September: *"The ultimate measure is how good Harbour is at completing work correctly."* And: *"Ideally, simple tasks get simple processes, complex tasks scale up. And things are left better than they were when the task started."*

The prompt came mid-passage. John asked how large a task is these days, and whether the process is overcomplicated or the tasks legitimately take this long. An audit of three landed tickets gave the answer: an 8-line inert change took 41 dispatches and an hour, while the credential cutover's review caught about seven real bugs that CI missed. The fleet is careful where it matters and heavy everywhere else. The research that followed says why.

## What we learned

The evidence is in the documents listed at the end. Figures are as corrected by the independent checks.

1. **The part that changes the product stayed the same size, and everything around it grew.**
   - Net product code has held at about 2,800 lines a week since the fleet started on 1 June.
   - Since then, test lines grew 10.6×, production comment lines 7.1×, and comment words per ticket went from about 1,600 to 6,000–7,500.
   - One unit-suite pass in CI takes 8–10× longer.
   - The fastest-growing code is the fleet's own machinery: Harbour's dispatch and fleet code grew 10×, and simple-dispatcher's hook and state machine 13×.
   - Process outgrows product from every starting week and under every definition tried. (`growth-atlas.md` v2)
2. **Most of a ticket's cost is fixed overhead, and most of that overhead is supervision.**
   - The supervision layers take 35% of the fleet's weighted tokens: the passage Runner, its legs, steppers, a ticket's own autopilot, and wakes.
   - Their September rise from 28% to 45% is **entirely the new passage layer** (the Runner and its legs). Without that layer, supervision was flat to falling (28%, 24%, 15% and 26% by week).
   - Median dispatches per ticket rose from 12 to 17 between July and September, 1.2–1.8× by size. Almost all of the rise is warm beats into sessions held open.
   - A docs- or tests-only ticket still costs about 77% of the median ticket's tokens. (`where-the-effort-goes.md` v2). On the backtest's census it used 64% of the median change's dispatches and 69% of its hours, and, held to its diff size, about the same dispatches as a production change.
   - At least half of a September standalone change's cost does not depend on its size, and probably most of it; a split family costs 1.8× its children as standalone changes. (`cost-mix.md` v2)
3. **Three-quarters of supervision is bookkeeping.**
   - 77% of supervision tokens go to steps fully determined by observable state: taking delivery of a wake, reading a row, answering the runner's completion gate, noting progress. That is about **27% of the fleet's weighted tokens**. A fresh blind recode puts the share higher, at 86% (81–91%), though the share rests on the codebook calling gate replies and fetches mechanical.
   - Judgement is concentrated in judging a worker's report and writing the next beat, about a fifth of the bill. Per ticket, the cycles in which a supervisor made a consequential decision are 12–20% of its cost; judgement concentrates at the gates and in the making. (`where-judgement-happens.md` v2)
   - **Nearly a third of wakes (31%) change nothing**, and they are 25% of the supervision bill. For the passage Runner, 94% of its wakes change nothing. Counted as every delivery into a held supervisor with its handshake and gate replies, the wakes that changed nothing are 16% of fleet tokens, 12% in classes code can see. (`held-or-fresh.md` v2)
   - Much of the answering is already held in deterministic code (the completion gate, wake delivery, stall handling), and part of the Done gate is in code; how much is unmeasured.
   - **Supervisor failures are mechanical, not judgement:** 22 of the 25 on record were in mechanical actions, on a reading of each ticket, and 21 were bugs in code. (`what-supervisors-do.md` v2) A relay of fresh sessions would remove 11 of the 25, mostly lost wakes; one recovery lived only in a held session's memory (LIN-2078). (`held-or-fresh.md` v2)
4. **This is why earlier fixes did not bend the curve.** Cheaper implementers and fewer generated tasks both worked on their targets. But implementation is about a quarter of the tokens, and the cheap tier carries under 0.1% of weighted units. An unworked ticket in the backlog costs almost nothing. The cost sits in supervision and checking, which are paid per ticket, per session and per wake, not per line changed.
5. **Reliability has held.**
   - LinearViewer's escaped-defect rate rose on paper, but September without review residue (faults the fleet's own reviews found in older code) is 5.7 per 100 merged PRs, against 5.4 in June.
   - simple-dispatcher's September is 27.3 per 100 without residue, within noise of June.
   - Review stops an estimated 35–45% of the bugs that are visible at all, concentrated in a few tickets: 22 real bugs in 9 tickets, mostly credential work.
   - Tickets that passed through more gates show no gradient in escaped defects.
   - Main is red on about 1% of pushes, and none of this year's three reverts was for a defect. (`reliability-baseline.md` v2)
6. **Effort barely follows risk, as far as we can tell.** At a fixed size, the data cannot show risk changing effort in either direction (intervals −8 to +14 dispatches). That is itself the finding: nothing in the process reliably sizes effort to risk.
7. **The tests mostly earn their keep. Their costs are friction, flakes and idling.**
   - Behavioural tests are 93% of Harbour's unit tests. On their own they kill most sampled logic mutants.
   - Pin-class tests (text pins, census pins, source scans) take about 5% of run time. As unit tests they turned CI red only twice since June; count pins inside browser specs did so three more times. They cost friction instead: pins demand bumps at least as often as they catch anything, on one or two catches a class, and about 18 tickets since June exist mainly to repair or bump a pin (about five are pure bumps).
   - The larger costs are elsewhere:
     - flaky browser specs are the biggest cause of red CI (unit-suite reds split evenly, 13 flakes and 13 real faults of 42);
     - 77% of the unit suite's serial time is spent outside any test;
     - **31% of it was four files idling about 25 seconds each on a timeout timer in `routes/proxy.js` that was never cleared**, costing 20–30 s of parallel wall-clock too. Fixed on 30 September (LIN-3158): the serial suite fell from 266 s to 175 s.
   - simple-dispatcher's mutation gate has two stale mutants and does not run in CI. (`test-estate.md` v2)
8. **Lessons accrue as prose and are rarely retired.**
   - 8 of the 10 tickets in the close-out lineage changed no runtime code.
   - 17 of 221 prompt-text commits left the text smaller.
   - "Merge and Done need a recorded Approve and a discharged ledger" is enforced only by prose. GitHub does enforce green CI.
   - Rule-bearing text is only about 3–5% of the context a session carries (3% at 4 bytes a token, 4–5% at the 2.6 that `starting-context.md` v2 measured). The cost is in what the rules make agents do. (`steady-base.md` v2, `steady-base-check.md`)
9. **Harbour throws its own evidence away.** Dispatch history, prompt traces, the app-call log and local session transcripts are kept for 30 days. No token series before September can be rebuilt.
10. **Single-session runs suit research, not changes to the system.**
    - Eleven papers and checks landed on 29–30 September, each as one bounded session, and none changed the product.
    - Changes to Harbour itself go through Harbour's process. An agent that once skipped it took a week to clean up after.
11. **June was a different regime, not a lost level.** *(`why-throughput-halved.md` v3)*
    - A correct change kept its size: 70 production lines at the median in June, 67 later. Every size band halved.
    - Most of the fall is fewer merged tickets (113 to 65 a week), and the rest a lower correct, complete share (84% to 72%): four-fifths and a fifth on the weeks of 8 June to 5 July, two-thirds and a third on the three weeks wholly in June. More than half of the escape rise is finder rows (faults the fleet's own reviews found in older code).
    - It is almost all Harbour's (86 to 39 a week), and a third to two-fifths of it is UI work (27 to 7 a week). Product lines fell by a third while test lines rose, so a correct change now carries 2.5× the test lines.
    - The step lines up with 12 July (LIN-1285), when each dispatch began choosing its tier; frontier-written changes fell from 84 a week to 12. The tier switch is the only change dated to the step: the plan-review leg arrived two weeks later (26 July), and test lines and prompts ramped up through July rather than stepping.
    - The "four times the escapes" claim did not survive re-derivation; see point 13.
12. **A few rules and reviewer judgement do the catching.** *(`which-rules-pay.md` v2)*
    - In the last 100 reviewed Done tickets (11–29 September), 483 review and close-out findings led to 78 production-code changes in 28 tickets; 44 fixed real faults, in 18 tickets, and 24 of those were in three tickets.
    - Reviewer judgement that no rule names led the most (29 changes, 16 faults), then the class check (19, 12), verifying against the requirements (10, 7) and looking at the running result (6, 3). The counts stand, but which rule led each change is the soft call: a blind recode names the same lead rule 59–66% of the time, and the class check's 19 may be high by about a third. Only the class check and requirements clearly separate from the zero rules.
    - 16 of 24 rules led no production change, and 12 never appeared on one even as support. About a third of those are gates, where a gate that works leaves no change behind. For most of the rest (record-keeping, filing, tests) zero is the design, and three rules existed for only the last six days.
    - The mutation check led 107 findings, 97 of which changed only tests or wording, and about 36 of the 88 extra legs.
    - Tickets touching only simple-dispatcher got no production change from review.
13. **Model tier is not what raised the cost.** *(`model-choice.md` v2)*
    - Until 12 July every session ran at the frontier tier, whatever the dispatch asked. Seven routing changes since are dated from the runner's logs, and implementation also ran at frontier on 23–27 July.
    - For changes merged 13 July–30 August, a correct, complete change cost about the same whole-life working hours whether a frontier session implemented it (2.9–3.0 h) or a mid-tier one (3.2–3.3 h), within noise (ratio 0.91, 95% 0.70–1.21). The implementer is read from the runner's sessions, not commit trailers, and the hours include the rework each change caused.
    - Mid-tier changes do escape more often: 13 of 302 against none of 60 in those weeks (p = 0.09), and 16 of 517 against none of 129 to 21 September (p = 0.03). But rework adds only 0.1–0.3 hours per change, because an escape's fix costs a fraction of the process around the original ticket. A quarter (frontier) to three-fifths (mid) of it lands in the first week, and a fifth to a quarter after day 30.
    - **What moved cost per change was the process, not the tier.** At the 12 July step, dispatches per correct change rose about two-and-a-half-fold at both tiers (frontier 7.9 to 18.6, mid 8.1 to 21.0), with every follow-up charged to the session it entered. In September's provisional weeks they are 52 (frontier), 32 (mid) and 37 (cheap).
    - The cheap tier and all of September are provisional until their 30-day windows close.
14. **What raised the dispatches: wakes into held autopilots.** *(`what-doubled-the-dispatches.md` v2)*
    - Counting every dispatch, a correct code change took 7.6 dispatches in the fortnight before 12 July and 20.0 in the fortnight after, in both repos.
    - Between 5 and 12 July fresh sessions rose, with no mechanism found, and follow-ups rose as cold resumes of finished sessions, which is what LIN-1219 does (a finished session is closed and a follow-up resumes it cold). Follow-ups per held session rose from 16 July, lining up with LIN-1357 (a wake for every stepper beat).
    - Since August, fresh sessions have been flat at about 11 per correct change. **The growth is wakes:** 8, then 10, 16 and 18 per change, each wake charged to the session it entered (29 if charged to the child the log names). The late-September rise sits on the tickets a passage flew (41 wakes per correct change against 14 on the rest), and wakes per worker event rose from 1.19 to 1.40.
    - September's dispatches per correct change against the map, by the session entered: quiet wakes 7%, the passage layer 8%, supervision that acted 38% (a ceiling), review rounds after the first plus test and CI beats at most 10%, and **36% in no candidate row** (by the log's line: 10%, 15%, 34%, 9% and 32%). The remainder is planning, review and close-out legs (a fifth of them repeats; repeat plan-reviews outnumber first ones), stepper beats inside plan and implementation sessions, and cheap-tier sessions whose kind cannot be read. Two parts of it are now sized: planned children's own plan and plan-review sessions are 3.9–4.3% of tokens (`step-overlap.md` v2), and orientation before a session's first productive call is 6–26% (`starting-context.md` v2).
15. **A lighter path for small work saves less than it looked.** *(`proportional-process-backtest.md` v2)*
    - Six size-and-risk rules were committed before any outcome was read, then run over 1,282 Done changes since June in both repos.
    - The docs-and-tests-only rule routes 11% of changes light, and none of its 89 mature light changes went wrong afterwards. The small-and-low-risk rule routes 26% light; 5 of its 249 went wrong (2 in 100).
    - The price: before merge, review caught real faults on 5 docs-only changes (parent tickets whose code shipped under children, a runbook, served prompt text) and 8 small low-risk ones, 13 of 17 on the merge-time groups at plan review.
    - A rule that reads only the ticket's text routes large, faulty changes light: 36 real faults were caught in its light group. Classification has to use the paths actually touched.
    - **Light work costs about half of heavy work because it is smaller;** held to the same diff size it takes about the same dispatches, so the process is not lighter on it today. The most a lighter process could save is what the light groups cost now: 13–18% of working hours and 21–30% of dispatches. The 77% was a per-ticket ratio, not a saving.
16. **Browser flakes are a few specs with three causes.** *(`browser-flakes.md` v2)*
    - There were no flakes before CI went to two Playwright workers on 24 June. Since then at least 64 of 138 red E2E attempts were flakes.
    - Seven specs carry every flake with a known test; `observation`, `observation-rulings` and `dispatch-presets` carry two-thirds.
    - The causes, read from the flaky tests' source, are waits and route holds that do not hold (15 of the 39 known-spec flakes), state left in a worker's own partition by earlier specs (11), and a read racing a re-render of the element (10). State shared between the two workers (LIN-625) explains June's burst only.
    - They cost 38 red PR runs, 54 re-runs, about 7½ hours end to end and about 3 runner-hours of re-runs. Retries hide more: 80% of sampled green runs passed a test only on an in-job retry.
17. **Every wake is minted in one place, and a third of them are relayed re-arms.** *(`wake-inventory.md` v2)*
    - Harbour creates every wake in `addFeedback` (when a child's feedback begins `[done]`, `[failed]`, `[blocked]` or `[pending]`), but aborts, halts and the runners' watchdogs also post the lines that mint them. simple-dispatcher delivers a wake warm into a held Stop hook or by a cold resume, re-delivers some it lost, and adds its own completion gate and stall re-fires; since 29 September LinearViewer's runner kit also delivers wakes, into a continued subagent. CI and PR results reach a session only through its own polls.
    - In September, terminal wakes led to an action 89% of the time; pause wakes changed nothing 83% of the time. **1,614 wakes (35%) were a supervisor's own PENDING-EXTERNAL re-arm relayed to its parent, and 97% of those were quiet.**
    - So one worker event sends 1.6 wakes up the chain outside a passage and 3.0 inside one. Per correct code change that is 18.9 wakes charged to the child's ticket (6.9 changing nothing), or 14.9 charged to the session each entered (3.4). Wakes took 29% of tokens, quiet wakes 12%.
    - 18 of the 25 supervisor failures on record are on a wake path, and 11 of the 15 lost wakes were lost in the runner's delivery, not in Harbour's minting. The record is a fraction of what happens: the wait graph found 27 lost wakes in September alone, 2 rescued by nothing, and the runner logs a failed `[done]` post as posted (22 of 23 since July) (`what-hides-between-sessions.md` v2).
18. **Legs repeat because a gate sent the work back, and what a repeat buys depends on its kind.** *(`why-legs-repeat.md` v2)*
    - 827 of the 2,252 plan, plan-review, review and close-out legs on Done tickets since 1 August are repeats, a few dozen fewer in fact (August's kinds are partly misread). In two blind-coded samples (144 repeats, two readers each, κ 0.89–1.00 on the reason), 121 of the 131 real repeats are the next round of a send-back loop; six re-ran a failed session, all on 2 September, one was a mistaken launch and one a rescue; none followed a stale grounding, added scope or red CI.
    - A repeated plan changes the plan's substance (24 of 34), and a repeated plan-review finds something real and new (30 of 41). A repeated code review mostly checks the fix (production code changed in 5 of 36), and later rounds' real faults, counted per PR, all sit on three tickets (13 of 13). **A repeated close-out changed nothing (20 of 20).**
    - In September the repeats are 3.1 dispatches per correct change: 7.8% of 39.4 by the log's line, 9.0% of 34.3 by the session entered; 10% of tokens and 6% of session-hours. Large tickets are the likeliest to take three or more rounds, though most tickets that do are smaller. A ruling precedes 70% of third planning rounds, as the loop bound requires, and 16% of third review rounds.
19. **A held supervisor's cost grows with its history; a fresh one is expensive to start.** *(`held-or-fresh.md` v2)*
    - A wake into a held supervisor costs a few thousand weighted tokens plus about 0.11 of its accumulated context, so its cost rises linearly with history. A fresh supervisor reads 116–238k tokens to its first decision, after a 62–82k bootstrap, in 0.7–2.1 minutes.
    - Modelled on September's 6,209 wakes, John's relay as proposed (code for the mechanical wakes, a fresh session for each acted one) costs about the same as today: 0% (−17% to +9%). Acted wakes made fresh cost 1.9× their held cost, and orientation is 60% of a fresh step.
    - It saves only with a small handoff: −19% as a bound, −9% with a 60k handoff. Supervisors' decisions rest on verdicts, plan text and code that a short note does not carry.
    - Sending quiet wakes to code, with acted wakes kept held, saves about 10% when code routes by the class it can see (−5.7% from row 1, −4.1% from the Runner, row 2), and 14% if code could tell in advance which wakes change nothing. 938 quiet wakes, 4.5% of fleet tokens, cannot be told apart by class.
    - In 48 sampled acted wakes, no decision rested on memory alone.
20. **Judgement changes outcomes at the gates and in the making, rarely in supervision.** *(`where-judgement-happens.md` v2)*
    - On 36 stratified September changes, about seven consequential decisions a change: 61–73% needed reasoning over context (by reader), at most 8% a rule over observable state, the rest a cheaper step. Fresh blind readers found fewer rule-class calls than the first reader (2–4%).
    - Plan review spends 59% of its cost in decision cycles, review 48% and close-out 45%; a ticket's own autopilot 7–10%, and half its cost is bookkeeping.
    - Of 163 wrong turns, one layer handled 83%. A fresh session holding only the record could have decided 82–91% of them. What needed more than one layer was a wrong premise or a false report, and John was in half of those.
21. **Sessions spend a lot getting their bearings, and some of it is re-finding.** *(`starting-context.md` v2, `step-overlap.md` v2)*
    - Orientation before a session's first productive call is 6–26% of weighted tokens across 2,015 September sessions, by how "productive" is defined. Re-finding what an earlier session on the same ticket had already read is 4.9–12.1%, and 85% of repeated file reads were of unchanged files. The bootstrap summary is 3.0%, plus up to 2.4% carried.
    - A short pointer beats a big pack: the paths in the prompt and ticket name about half an implementer's edited files at about 60% precision, while broad methods name about 100 files at about 5%. One probe (LIN-2115) found a 93-token pointer cut a task from 51 turns to 18.
    - Steps paraphrase rather than copy. A plan restates about a fifth to a quarter of the research and is about 70% new; plan review re-verifies a quarter to a third, as its prompt asks. Research is used three times in four and cited one time in four to six. Ticket reads are 1.3–2.4% of carried context.
    - The duplication is in process, not text: planned children running their own plan and plan review take 3.9–4.3% of tokens.
22. **Most of the budget goes where review rarely catches anything, and the risky work sets a ceiling.** *(`cost-mix.md` v2)*
    - September's 3.6B weighted tokens: proxy, dispatch and fleet machinery 27% (about 10 points of it rulings, Flight Companion and scan work), work that merged no change of its own 22%, docs and tests 18% (the survey papers 10%), credentials and auth 10%. About 13–14M weighted tokens per correct change, and about one weekly allowance a week.
    - All 43 coded real review catches in the last 100 reviewed tickets were in changes of 50 or more lines; none was in the 23 small or docs-only ones (a small sample: no catch in 23 has a probability of 0.43–0.61). Escapes run 1.3, 2.1 and 2.2 per 100 by size band, without residue; credential work escapes at 5.1%.
    - **The bound on correct work per budget,** holding credential work at today's rigour: everything else three times cheaper gives 2.5×, ten times cheaper 5.2×, and free about 10×; also holding fleet machinery, 2.8× (about 3.7× without its rulings and scan work). The paper's options 1–4 together come to about 1.7–1.8×.
23. **Process changes have landed often, been measured rarely and seldom finished.** *(`how-process-changes-land.md` v2)*
    - Of 27 notable process changes since June, 2 were measured before and after by design, 9 are still half-finished, and none came with a condition for retiring it; the one sunset written into a change (LIN-2323) was never read. Additions outnumber removals about six to one (95% interval about 2:1 to 21:1).
    - The process code holds 47 switches, kept old paths and parked experiments; the tracker holds 100 open process follow-ups and 114 review-residue tickets, at a median of 53 days.
    - No past change's effect can be isolated: each landed within four weeks of 5–16 others.
    - Trials: a before-and-after sees ×2.1 at four weeks a side; a holdout by ticket ×1.8–1.9 in dispatches and ×1.5 in hours (×2.1–2.7 and ×1.6–1.9 if changes under one supervisor move together); neither sees a saving under ×1.3 within eight weeks. Shadow mode measures agreement, not cost.
24. **A lean pipeline on small work is much cheaper; whether it is as correct is not known.** *(`replay-small-work.md` v2, `survey-check-11.md`)*
    - Pre-registered, 13 small low-risk Done tickets were replayed in isolated worktrees by one mid-tier implementer, one review and one close-out. They cost 4.1% of the originals' session tokens (5.4% at matched tiers). A lean lane in production would cost about 5–10% of today's in a lean harness, and 18–24% with today's per-leg tracker and chore habits.
    - The weight is in the legs' harness and habits, not the model: one fleet leg's orientation (a 77k bootstrap, 115k before a fresh review's first decision) exceeds a whole replay leg (20–56k).
    - Correctness is unmeasured. Blind reads favoured the replay, but 8 of 13 descriptions carry what shipped. Replayed from the text as it stood before implementation, two of four verdicts moved from better to worse. By the registered verdict the replay was 5 better, 1 equivalent and 7 worse. On the five known faults it mostly missed what the full process missed; three of five passed every review, lean or full. Only a forward trial can say whether lean is as correct.
25. **Many failures are invisible to any one session, and simple code would have seen them sooner.** *(`what-hides-between-sessions.md` v2)*
    - The tracker holds 107 incidents of nine cross-session patterns since June; 92 were invisible to any single session. They surfaced a median of 16 hours after onset (3 hours where the onset is timed). Supervisors, workers, John and the Flight Companion found them; the periodicals' only first find was their own outage (LIN-3104).
    - Six rules over data Harbour and the runner already hold caught 9 of 19 scored incidents (two of them, 7), a median of 5 hours before they were filed, including LIN-3181 and the 1 October circular wait. They also raised alarms on about 40 real faults that are on no ticket, for under a minute of CPU and no model tokens.
    - The cost is idle time, not tokens: in September, 42 hours of supervisors waiting on lost wakes and 165 hours of stalls; outside quiet wakes, about 1.6% of tokens.
26. **John's prototypes agree with the measurements but add no sizes.** *(`prototype-concepts.md` v2)*
    - A short pointer, not a maintained map: Pith's code map lost to exploration on 14 of 15 tasks, and CodeWiki's answers did not improve as it grew.
    - Code holding the sequence around a narrow model call has the best support (Browser-agent, 3/20 to 19/20, on a small model).
    - A durable record alone does not stop repeated work: a planner given one re-proposed done work for about half its nodes (tag-two).
    - Lighthouse, a lean research process with a second-agent review, costs about the same per piece as a one-session Harbour paper. Review in both keeps finding corrections: about 20 of 24 Harbour checks corrected a load-bearing claim.

## What this implies

- **The largest saving is mechanical supervision, and it keeps every altitude.** About 27% of fleet tokens go to supervision steps that observable state fully decides. A supervisor keeps its role (context isolation, judgement of reports, the next beat) while code does the waiting, re-arming, polling, restating and gate-answering it does today in prose. The failure record points the same way: supervisors fail in the plumbing, not in judgement. Starting each judgement step fresh is not a saving while fresh sessions orient as they do today; it pays only with a small handoff (point 19).
- **The wake plumbing is where the growth is.** Fresh sessions per change have been flat since August; wakes per change more than doubled (8 to 18 per correct change). Every wake is minted in one place, and 35% of them are a supervisor relaying its own "still waiting" upward, almost all quiet. How often supervisors are woken, and how far up each wake travels, is a narrower and more mechanical target than "the process". Code routing wakes by the class it can see reaches about 10% of fleet tokens (point 19).
- **Proportionality is a real but smaller lever.** Sized by the paths touched, a lighter path could save 13–18% of working hours (21–30% of dispatches) at a price of about 2 escapes in 100 light changes and some faults review now catches before merge. It must classify on code, not ticket text. In correct work per weekly budget it is about 1.1× for a light lane on small non-credential changes and 1.2× for gates sized to the change (`cost-mix.md` v2).
- **Reliability is steady, so the baseline is clean.** We are not fixing a quality problem. We are removing cost that quality does not depend on, and the scorecard will say if we are wrong.
- **Tests: fix the idling and the flakes first, loosen pins second.** The idling is fixed. The flakes are the biggest cause of red CI. The pins are friction, not run time.
- **Choosing cheaper models is not the lever.** Whole-life cost per change is about the same at frontier and mid tier; what raised dispatches per change about two-and-a-half-fold at the 12 July step, for every tier, was the process around the change. That is the process, which is where the map below aims.
- **Review's value is concentrated.** A few rules and reviewer judgement make the production catches, mostly on a few risky tickets. That supports sizing the process to the change, and it says which checks a lighter path must keep. The mutation check's test-only rounds are the clearest candidate cost.
- **What a session reads before it acts is a lever of a few percent,** bounded by re-finding (4.9–12.1%). The bootstrap summary (up to ~5%) and planned children re-planning (up to ~4%) are the clearest cuts; a short pointer, not a large pack, is what the evidence supports (point 21).
- **The risky work sets a ceiling.** Holding credential work at today's rigour, the bound on correct work per budget is about 2.5× if everything else gets three times cheaper and about 10× if it were free (point 22). A doubling needs the structural rows (1–3 and the relay's handoff) as well as proportionality; proportionality alone gives about 1.1–1.2×.
- **How a change lands matters as much as what it is.** Past changes were rarely measured, often left half-finished and never given a retirement condition, and additions outran removals six to one (point 23). Each change in this work needs a finish line, a measurement planned before it lands, and a condition for retiring it.
- **Small work may be an order of magnitude cheaper, and its correctness is the open question.** A lean pipeline cost about a twentieth of today's process on small low-risk tickets, and the weight is in each leg's harness and habits rather than the model (point 24). Only a forward trial, measured on the scorecard's correct rate, can say whether it is as correct.
- **Code detects, the model decides.** Failures that no single session can see surface hours late and cost idle time; simple rules over held data see many of them early (point 25). Detection belongs with the liveness clocks in row 3; deciding what to do stays with a model.
- **Harbour changes itself through its own process,** measured against the baseline below.

## How we will measure it

The instrument is `scripts/survey-scorecard.mjs`, defined in `measuring-throughput.md`.
- **A change** is a ticket whose work merged and reached Done.
- **Correct** means no escaped Bug and no named fix commit points at it within 30 days.
- **Complete** means it filed none of its own work as a follow-up.
- **Throughput** is correct, complete changes per week, per fleet dispatch and per working hour. Process weight added or removed is recorded per change.

**Baseline:**
- about **47 correct, complete changes a week since mid-July** (95 a week in the weeks of 8 to 29 June, 86 in the three weeks wholly in June, 69 in June's calendar weeks; June was a different regime, see point 11);
- **26–49 dispatches and 2.7–3.7 working hours per correct change** (July's 4.4 counted other workspaces' sessions);
- 11–16M weighted tokens each in September (about 14M for the month, about 8% less at list-price ratios, because the September mid tier is weighted at 0.6 of the frontier tier and listed at 0.4; the weighted unit also charges both frontier price rows at 1, though per weighted token the newer row, the fleet's from the week of 22 September, lists at about 0.55 of the older, so a list-price series falls there where the weighted one does not (`survey-check-10.md`)); about one weekly allowance a week (`cost-mix.md` v2).

**What a doubling looks like:** it shows first as **halved cost per correct change**. Over four weeks the instrument detects a 2.8× shift in dispatches per change or 2.0× in hours, so hours per change sees a doubling at about four weeks. On the weekly ratio, a before-and-after sees ×2.1 at four weeks a side; a holdout by ticket sees ×1.8–1.9 in dispatches and ×1.5 in hours (×2.1–2.7 and ×1.6–1.9 if changes under one supervisor move together); neither sees a saving under ×1.3 within eight weeks (`how-process-changes-land.md` v2). The weekly count needs about eight weeks either side. A change counts as a saving only if cost per correct change falls and the correct rate holds.

## How it fits V1

V1 is one person, one task, one proven merge (`docs/v1.md`). A stranger's task runs through this same pipeline, so the overhead is part of the product they experience. `docs/v1.md` lists "what a run should cost" as an open hypothesis, and the steady-base work is how Harbour answers it. The natural slot is between V1's dress rehearsal and the invited period: prove V1 works, then make it proportional before other people use it. The quick wins above can land sooner as ordinary tickets without touching V1's structure.

## Open questions

- **What is the right shape for Harbour's process,** not just a lighter version of today's? This needs John's thought as well as the data.
- **Should a change's cost include the wakes into the epics and Runner above it?** The answer moves September's figures by about a third (`survey-check-4.md`); on the wake inventory's cohort it is 18.9 wakes per correct change by the child against 14.9 by the session entered (`survey-check-5.md`). There are four rules (child named, session entered, lineage spread, pooled), and which is fair depends on where a change saves (`how-process-changes-land.md` v2). On code changes merged 14–28 September the first two differ by 30% in `what-doubled-the-dispatches.md`'s census and 15% in `how-process-changes-land.md`'s, and the two censuses are not yet reconciled (`survey-check-9.md`). The wake lever shows as 10–14% of fleet tokens but about 3% of a sampled ticket's cost, because most quiet wakes land in sessions above the ticket (`survey-check-7.md`). So the scorecard needs one rule, and one census, before the epic is measured.
- **What is the rest of the unexplained remainder?** Repeat legs explain about 15% of it, 1.9 of 12.8 per correct change; their other 1.1 is in row 8 (`survey-check-6.md`). First-round legs and stepper beats inside sessions are the bulk; 29% of a stepper's tokens are re-orientation at the start of a beat, and what a supervisor's later beat re-reads is mostly the ticket (`starting-context.md` v2, `survey-check-8.md`).
- **Which lessons behind today's rules are still earning their keep?** `which-rules-pay.md` gives the first firing record: 8 of 24 rules have led a production change. Rasmussen's warning applies: a defence's value is invisible until it is removed, and about a third of the zero rules are gates; for most of the rest, zero is the design.
- **Which deliveries into a held supervisor can code tell are quiet before a model reads them?** 938 quiet wakes, 4.5% of fleet tokens, cannot be separated by class (`survey-check-7.md`).
- **Can the ~2× aim be met with credential and fleet work held at today's rigour?** The bound says yes in principle (2.5× at three times cheaper elsewhere), but proportionality and sized gates alone give 1.7–1.8× at most; the structural rows have to deliver (`cost-mix.md` v2, `survey-check-9.md`).
- **Failures that no single session can see** (a dead credential healed every 30 seconds for 11 hours, LIN-3181; a circular wait that idled a passage leg on 1 October) suggested a principle: code detects, the model decides. The sixth wave sized it (point 25): 107 incidents since June, 92 invisible to one session, a median 16 hours to discovery; six rules over held data caught 9 of 19 scored a median 5 hours early. The cost is hours, not tokens.
- **Is a lean lane on small work as correct as today's process?** The replay says it is far cheaper and cannot say whether it is as correct; only a forward trial on the scorecard can (`survey-check-11.md`).
- **Every paper cited here has been checked by a second document.** `survey-check-9.md`, `survey-check-8.md` and `survey-check-7.md` checked the fifth wave's six papers; `survey-check-10.md` and `survey-check-11.md` checked the sixth wave's three.
