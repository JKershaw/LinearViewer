# Incident — sustained Linear 401 flood on the proxy lane

**Date:** 2026-08-09
**Duration:** 09:26:31 – 09:57:32 UTC (≈31 min)
**Severity:** Degraded — autopilot agents could not read issues, and stopped. No data loss, no writes affected.
**Status:** Root cause **NOT** identified. Amplifier identified; fix built and held (see *Holding the fix*).

> **All times below are UTC.** An earlier revision of this document mixed a
> local-clock (BST, UTC+1) screenshot into a UTC timeline and overstated the
> duration as ≈75 min. See *Corrections* at the end — one hypothesis leaned on
> that mistake.

---

## What happened

Production emitted a sustained wall of Linear `401 AUTHENTICATION_ERROR` responses at
roughly one per second, for about half an hour, on the headless proxy lane. Dispatched
agents could not read issues, and eventually stopped calling. The dispatch queue backed
up behind them.

```
Proxy /issue error: Authentication required, not authenticated:
  {"response":{"errors":[{"extensions":{"code":"AUTHENTICATION_ERROR","statusCode":401,
   "userPresentableMessage":"You need to authenticate to access this operation."}}],
   "status":401}, "request":{"variables":{"id":"266f0841-…"}}}
```

The critical property: **this was not a resolution failure.** `resolveWorkspaceAccess`
succeeded, returned a token it believed live, routed to the Linear provider, and
*Linear* rejected the credential. A `WORKSPACE_NOT_CONNECTED` 503 would have meant the
opposite — that no token was found. Most of the investigation time was lost to chasing
the wrong branch, because nothing in the logs distinguished them at a glance.

| — | — | Operator's own proxy token reads the *same* failing issue ids at **200** |

**Why it ended.** The final call is `/me` — the `viewer` query, the canonical "is my
credential still valid?" probe. The pattern is an agent 401ing on reads, probing `/me`
to check, seeing 401, concluding its credential is dead, and stopping. **The callers
gave up; the system did not recover.** The dispatch queue corroborates it: 22 `wake`
items sat at `feedbackCount: 0`, never claimed.

This matters for reading finding (3) below: nothing repaired the credential. The error
stream stopped because the traffic stopped, and the cost moved from a visible error
wall to silently stalled work.

**The re-add is not timestamped**, so it cannot be ordered against 09:57:32. If it
landed just before, it — not the agents giving up — may be what ended the flood. The
two are distinguishable in the audit log (`/api/proxy/events`) and were not separated.

Deployed commit throughout: `fc0a4c84`. No deploy occurred during the incident window.
The only change authored that day (`717f2f7`, the `write:jira-work` scope) was never
deployed and is not implicated.

## What is established

1. **The workspace's Linear credential was never globally dead.** The same issue ids
   that 401'd for the agents (LIN-1542 … LIN-1545) returned 200 for an operator token
   in the same window, on the same endpoint. The failure was **credential-specific**.
2. **Token selection admits revoked credentials.** `selectOwnerWorkspaceToken`
   (`lib/workspace-token-resolver.js:113`) admits any token whose `tokenExpiresAt` is
   more than five minutes in the future. A token *revoked upstream* is indistinguishable
   from a healthy one until the provider answers 401.
3. **There is no self-heal on the headless lane.** `evictWorkspaceToken` is called from
   zero places in `routes/proxy.js`. `graphqlErrorStatus` maps the upstream 401 → 401
   (`routes/proxy.js:963`), returns it, and stops. The stored credential and its 30s
   cache entry are untouched, and the next poll re-serves the identical dead credential.
   `handleUnauthorizedError` — which *does* refresh, re-mint, or prompt a re-link — is
   on the session/web path only. A headless 401 teaches the system nothing.
4. **Refresh-on-resolve cannot fire for this failure mode.** It is gated on
   `!selected.token` (`server.js:1791`), reachable only when *no* token resolved. A
   dead-but-unexpired token sails past it.

(3) and (4) together fully explain the *persistence*: once a stored credential is
dead-but-unexpired, the lane re-serves it on every request, for as long as anything
keeps polling. That is the solid red wall, and it is why nothing the operator did from a
phone stopped it.

Stated precisely, because an earlier revision overreached here: this does **not** mean
the errors would have continued forever. They stopped at 09:57:32 — because the callers
stopped, not because Harbour healed. The credential was never repaired, evicted, or
refreshed by anything in the system. The failure mode is therefore not "an unbounded
error stream" but "agents are taken offline and the work silently stalls", which is
harder to notice, not easier.

## What is NOT established

**The trigger.** Nothing above says what killed the credential in the first place.

One **open, unconfirmed** candidate: `linkProvider` (`lib/workspace.js:312-327`)
mirrors a newly-linked provider's token *and its expiry* onto the workspace's scalar
fields whenever `workspace.provider` is unset — which is the legacy state for a Linear
workspace, since absent-provider is read as Linear throughout. Selection is then by
**maximum `tokenExpiresAt`, not recency**, so a mirrored never-expires sentinel
(`Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER`, used by Jira Basic and GitHub-family bindings) would win
selection permanently — unbeatable by a token refresh, and unbeatable by re-connecting
the workspace, since a fresh Linear token's `now+24h` always loses.

**This hypothesis is weaker than the first revision of this document claimed**, and the
correction matters more than the hypothesis:

- *The timing evidence was cross-clock.* It was originally stated as "Jira linked at
  09:22, first 401 at 09:26:31 — four minutes apart." The 09:22 was a **local** time
  mentioned in conversation (and about the current time, not the link); the 09:26:31 is
  **UTC** from the logs. Converted, the link was roughly an hour *before* onset, not
  four minutes. **The correlation the hypothesis was built on does not hold.**
- *The counter-evidence is unresolved.* The same code path also sets
  `workspace.provider` to `jira`, which should route reads to JiraProvider. The logs
  show a **Linear** GraphQL query throughout. Either this is not the mechanism, or the
  provider field and the token diverged across session rows.

What *does* survive is the one same-clock comparison: the LIN-1970 `review` dispatch at
**09:23:29** and the first error at **09:26:31**, three minutes apart, both system
timestamps. That is a genuine correlation — though a dispatch starting an agent that
then begins polling is an equally good explanation for it as anything causal, since the
agent's first read is simply the first time the dead credential would be exercised.

The hypothesis is therefore **open, not leading.** It is retained because the code path
is real and reachable and the diagnostics now test for it directly, not because the
evidence points at it.

Three hypotheses were **ruled out** by reading the code and should not be re-investigated:
- Global Linear outage or workspace-wide revocation — refuted by the 200s above.
- Durable-store cross-provider overwrite — `OwnerCredentialStore.put` partitions
  correctly on `(accountId, urlKey, provider)`.
- `linkProvider` clobbering a workspace already marked `linear` — the `isActive` guard
  blocks it. (The *unset*-provider hole above is a different, still-open case.)

## Why it took so long

The logs could not answer any of the questions that would have identified this:

- Which credential did this call use? *(Unanswerable — nothing identified one.)*
- Where did it come from — cache, session scan, or refresh?
- What expiry did the server believe it had?
- Was the credential even the right *shape* for the resolved provider?
- Was this a bad **caller** token or a bad **workspace** credential? Both are 401 with
  opposite remedies, and both logged identically.

A secondary cost: early in the incident the on-call agent probed production with **its
own** working token, got 200s across the board, and reported the system healthy. That
was a bad test — it measured the one credential known to work while the failing ones
went unmeasured — and it delayed escalation. Probing with a credential that is not the
failing credential proves nothing.

## Fix shipped

Diagnostics only. `lib/credential-diagnostics.js` (pure, secret-safe) plus wiring at the
proxy lane's two chokepoints — the resolution site (`resolveProviderAccess`) records a
descriptor, the status site (`logEvent`) emits it on any 401.

Each 401 now emits one `[credential-rejected]` line carrying:

| Field | Answers |
|---|---|
| `stage` | caller's bearer token vs. stored workspace credential |
| `credentialFingerprint` | *which* credential — correlatable across replicas, one-way |
| `credentialSource` | `cache` / `session-scan` / `refresh-on-resolve` |
| `expiryKind` + `msUntilExpiry` | `finite` / **`sentinel`** / `absent` |
| `credentialShape` + `shapeMismatch` | a cross-provider credential, flagged on sight |
| `provider` | distinguishes explicitly-`linear` from **unset** (the clobber-exposed case) |
| `proxyTokenId` / `ownerAccountId` | which agent, whose owner stamp |

`shapeMismatch` and `expiryKind: 'sentinel'` are the two fields that would confirm or
kill the `linkProvider` hypothesis on the next occurrence, without any further
investigation.

No token bytes are logged, in any field or shape — asserted over the whole serialized
payload so a field added later cannot quietly leak. Deliberately not rate-limited: a
flood of identical fingerprints is precisely the evidence that one stuck credential is
being re-served, and throttling would erase it.

## Follow-ups — NOT done here

1. **No 401 invalidation on the headless lane.** The amplifier is diagnosed but not
   fixed. A provider 401 should evict the cached credential and force re-resolution
   (with backoff), so a dead credential cannot be re-served indefinitely. *This is the
   fix that would have bounded the incident to seconds.*
2. **Selection admits revoked credentials.** Unexpired ≠ valid. Worth recording a
   last-known-rejected marker so selection can skip a credential the provider just
   refused.
3. **`linkProvider`'s unset-provider mirror.** Confirm or refute the clobber against
   stored data, then close the hole.
4. **Sentinel expiry beats everything, permanently.** Max-expiry selection means a
   never-expires credential can never be displaced by a healthy one. Selection should
   probably prefer a provider-matched credential over a merely longer-lived one.
5. **`resolveWorkspaceForTitles`** (`lib/workspace-title-resolver.js`) scans every
   session doc in the database, is owner-blind and provider-blind, and swallows its
   errors. It produced the incident's first symptom. Not touched here.

## Corrections to this document

Recorded rather than silently edited, because both errors were reasoning errors in the
investigation, not typos — and the second one shaped a conclusion.

1. **Duration was overstated as ≈75 min; it was ≈31 min** (09:26:31 – 09:57:32 UTC).
   A Railway screenshot timestamped 10:30–10:40 was read as UTC and treated as the tail
   of the incident. It was **local** time (BST, UTC+1) — 09:30–09:40 UTC — which sits
   in the *middle* of the window, not past its end.

2. **The leading hypothesis rested on a cross-clock correlation.** "Jira linked at
   09:22, first 401 at 09:26:31" compared a local time against a UTC one. Converted,
   the gap is about an hour, not four minutes. The hypothesis was downgraded from
   *leading* to *open* once this surfaced.

3. **"Would not have stopped on its own" was too strong.** The lane re-serves a dead
   credential for as long as anything polls; it does not generate errors forever. The
   flood ended when the callers gave up.

The general lesson is narrow and worth keeping: **never compare a human-reported time
to a log timestamp without converting first.** Two of the three errors above are the
same mistake. The timeline table now records clock provenance per row so the comparison
cannot be made implicitly again.

There is a second-order lesson about the first one. The incorrect duration was not
load-bearing on its own — but it was cited as supporting evidence for the hypothesis in
the same breath as the cross-clock link time, which made a weakly-supported theory read
as a well-corroborated one. Wrong facts in an incident log do not stay decorative; they
get recruited.
