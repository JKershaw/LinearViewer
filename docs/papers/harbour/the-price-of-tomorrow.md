# The Price of Tomorrow

*AI investment, technological progress, and the long journey from capability to economic value*

Prepared for John Kershaw | October 2026

Financial markets have priced artificial intelligence as though new capability becomes profit
for the people who build it, and becomes it soon. The record to October 2026 does not yet
support that, but it does not support a crash either. It shows four things moving at four
different speeds. The capability of the models compounds. The cost of getting a piece of
useful, checked work out of them falls much more slowly, and nobody publishes it. Use spreads
wide and stays shallow. National productivity sits on its long-run trend. Where value can be
measured, most of it goes to the people using AI, not to the companies selling it or the
investors funding the data centres. The likeliest outcome is therefore not one verdict but a
divided one. Some markets, chiefly the financing of compute, are over-invested relative to the
returns their owners can capture. Some applications will turn out to be worth little. Some
uses, narrow ones where the output can be checked, are already transformative. And the
financial timeline and the diffusion timeline are not as separable as the question that
commissioned this essay assumed. They are coupled through the price and supply of compute,
but not through the slower business of organisations learning to use what they have bought.

The question John put was whether this is a technological revolution whose financial
expectations have arrived too early, or an investment boom whose promised economic revolution
will never fully arrive. The answer here is that both can be true at once, in different layers
of the same economy, and that the next four years can tell them apart if the right things are
measured. That last part is the essay's most practical contribution, and it closes the body.

A note on reading. Every claim taken from a source carries a bracketed key, such as [CEN26],
into the annotated reading list, which says what each source supports, what it does not, and
which of four labels it carries. *Observed* means a published measurement, an experiment or
established history. *Inferred* means a reading that explains several observations but is not
causally established. *Hypothesised* means a proposed mechanism, usually where data is
missing. *Predicted* means a development that would support or contradict a hypothesis. In the
body, a keyed fact is Observed unless it says otherwise; the other three labels are written
where they apply. This is the sequel to [Learning While the Tools Change](learning-while-the-tools-change.md)
[H-LWT], which argued that experience, skill with AI and the tools themselves run on separate
clocks. This essay moves the same question from a team of developers to the economy.

## 1 The boom

### What was built

Start with what can be counted. The Stanford AI Index puts global corporate investment in AI
at $14.6 billion in 2013 and $581.7 billion in 2025, with private investment alone at $344.7
billion, up 127.5% in a year [AII26]. That is thirteen annual points from one source, and the
cleanest long series in this essay; its earlier editions revise their own back-series, so it
should be read from one edition, not spliced.

The physical build is clearer still. The Census Bureau has counted data-centre construction
separately from offices since 2014. In 2014 the United States put $1.8 billion of data-centre
structures in place; in 2025, $49.7 billion; and in August 2026 the monthly figure ran at an
annual rate of $85.0 billion, 73% above August 2025 [CEN26]. Those are buildings, not chips:
the servers and accelerators inside them are counted elsewhere.

![US data-centre construction in billions of dollars a year, 2014 to 2025, with August 2026 at an annual rate](figures/the-price-of-tomorrow/data-centre-construction.svg)

The chips show up in company accounts. Nvidia's data-centre segment earned $89.0 billion in
the quarter to July 2026, up 117% on a year earlier, at a 75% gross margin [NVDA26].
Alphabet spent $51.4 billion on property and equipment in the third quarter of 2026 alone
[GOOG26]. Microsoft spent $41 billion in its quarter to June and said "customer demand
continues to exceed available capacity" [MSFT26]. Meta spent $34.9 billion in the September
quarter and guided to $135–145 billion for 2026 [META26].

At the scale of the whole economy, the St. Louis Fed estimates that AI-related categories
(information-processing equipment, software, research and development, and data centres)
contributed 0.97 percentage points to US real growth in the first three quarters of 2025, 39%
of all growth, against 0.81 points and 28% in 2000 [STL26]. That is a gross figure. Much of the
equipment is imported, and the BEA's first estimate of the third quarter of 2026 names capital
goods as the largest contributor to imports in the same release in which it names
information-processing equipment as the leader of equipment investment [BEA26]. So the boom's
contribution to American output is smaller than its contribution to American spending.

### Where the bubble argument is strongest

The bubble case is not that the numbers are large. It is in how the spending is paid for and
what is being paid for it.

Until 2026 the standard reply to "bubble" was that the largest spenders paid from their own
cash flow, unlike the debt-funded telecom carriers of 1999. That reply weakened this year.
Alphabet's free cash flow was negative $5.9 billion in the second quarter of 2026 and negative
$9.1 billion in the third [GOOG26]. Meta's was negative $2.1 billion in the third quarter, when
capital spending of $34.9 billion exceeded operating cash flow of $32.8 billion [META26]. A
week later Meta raised $37.5 billion of new equity: $25 billion through a forward sale of
common stock at $625 a share and $12.5 billion of mandatory convertible preferred [META26b].
The BIS says the five largest hyperscalers' AI spending across 2025 and 2026, over a trillion
dollars, is "outpacing earnings and the free cash flow of these firms" [BIS26].

The financing has moved into credit markets. The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee,
citing Morgan Stanley, recorded about $450 billion of AI-related debt issued in 2026 by early
September, more than double the whole of 2025, and estimates that $700 billion of data-centre
spending over 2026–28 would be financed by private credit; JPMorgan's estimate is $4.1 trillion
of debt-financed AI capital spending over 2026–30 [BOE26]. Goldman Sachs, which on balance
argued against a bubble, still noted a year earlier that "entities are being funded with 80%
debt and 20% equity" [GS25]. Debt changes what a disappointment does. An equity loss is
absorbed by the people who chose the risk; a debt loss is passed along to whoever lent.

The revenue at the frontier is real and rising, but it is run-rate revenue set against
long-dated commitments. OpenAI's chief financial officer reported annualised revenue above $20
billion in 2025, from $6 billion in 2024, as its compute rose from 0.6 to 1.9 gigawatts
[OAI26a]. A company presentation seen by the Financial Times projects revenue of $36 billion in
2026 rising to $350 billion in 2030, against about $856 billion of spending on computing power
and $278 billion of cumulative negative cash flow from 2026 to 2030 [OAI26b]. Anthropic
reported run-rate revenue above $47 billion in May 2026, when it raised $65 billion at a $965
billion valuation [ANT26]. These are private figures reported by the companies or the press,
not audited accounts, and a run-rate is not a year's booked revenue. The bet they describe is
that revenue grows about tenfold in four years and that the people who paid for the compute
are the ones who collect it.

Sequoia's David Cahn put the gap in one number in 2024: take Nvidia's revenue, double it for
the full cost of a data centre, double again for the margin the end user needs, and the
industry needed $600 billion a year of revenue it did not have. He expected the shortfall to
"cause harm primarily to investors" while founders benefited from cheaper compute [SEQ24].
That is the bubble argument at its strongest. It is not that the technology fails. It is that
the people financing it may not be the people paid by it.

### The case that it is not as overvalued as it looks

The opposite case is also strong, and it rests on the public companies rather than the private
ones. In October 2025 the median forward price-to-earnings ratio of the seven largest US
technology stocks was 25, about half that of the seven largest companies at the peak of the
dot-com bubble; US AI investment over the previous twelve months was below 1% of GDP, against
roughly 1.5–2% at the peaks of 1920s electrification and the late-1990s IT boom and 2–5% for
historic infrastructure booms [GS25]. Goldman Sachs is a seller of securities and its report
should be read as an argued position, but the arithmetic is checkable.

Revenue at the supply edge is growing faster than in most bubbles. Nvidia's data-centre
revenue more than doubled in a year [NVDA26]. Google Cloud's revenue rose 103% to $30.7
billion in a quarter and earned an operating profit [GOOG26]. Microsoft reports demand above
capacity [MSFT26]. A boom in which buyers are queueing is not the same as one in which
capacity sits idle.

History supplies a caution against the word "bubble" itself. The Richmond Fed's account of the
telecom boom found real investment in communications equipment rising from about $62 billion
a year in early 1996 to over $135 billion by the end of 2000, then falling to 4.8% of private
investment by 2002 from nearly 7% in 2000. Its authors explicitly declined to call it a bubble
that burst: prices and investment had been based on beliefs about future demand that turned
out wrong in a period of rapid technical and regulatory change [CHW03]. A forecasting error on
that scale can destroy as much capital as a bubble. It is still a different mechanism, and it
predicts something different afterwards: the cable stayed in the ground.

The BIS, which is not given to optimism, makes the same distinction in reverse. Its 2026 report
groups the canal mania, the railway mania, 1920s electrification and the dot-com boom as
episodes that "all shared one common trait: a genuine technological breakthrough that attracted
capital in excess of what commercial returns could ultimately justify" [BIS26]. The breakthrough
was real each time. The returns were not where the capital went looking for them.

### Value created is not value captured

That phrase, returns not where the capital looked, is the centre of the economic argument, and
it runs through every later section. William Nordhaus estimated that only "a minuscule fraction"
of the social returns from technological advances in the United States between 1948 and 2001
was captured by the firms that produced them; most of the benefit went to consumers [NOR04].
That is a finding from another era about the whole economy, not about AI, and it should be
read as a base rate for the mechanism, not as an estimate.

For AI there is one direct attempt to measure the user side. Brynjolfsson and colleagues asked
representative samples of US adults how much they would need to be paid to give up chatbots
for a month. The aggregate came to $116 billion in 2025 and $172 billion in 2026, which the
authors say "substantially exceeds" estimated US generative-AI revenue [BCE26]. This is stated
preference, not observed spending; the mean is $124.50 a month while the median is $11.40, so
a few heavy users carry most of it. But it points the same way as Nordhaus. *Inferred*: so far,
the value of generative AI to its users is larger than what its sellers collect, and the gap
is the normal shape for a general-purpose technology, not a sign that the value is imaginary.

Capture also splits inside the supply side. Nvidia earns a 75% gross margin on the chips
[NVDA26]; the model providers project years of negative cash flow [OAI26b]; the data-centre
owners are financing with debt [BOE26]. Whoever holds the scarce input collects; whoever
competes to sell a commodity on top of it does not. A technology can therefore be
transformative while most of its investors earn disappointing returns, and the two statements
are not in tension.

## 2 The missing productivity

The experimental evidence for task-level gains is solid and narrow. In a staggered rollout to
5,179 customer-support agents at one firm, an AI assistant raised issues resolved per hour by
14% on average and by 34% for novice and low-skilled workers [BLR23]. Similar results exist for
writing, consulting and coding tasks. They measure a task done faster, inside one organisation,
over months.

The evidence against easy generalisation is as solid. METR's 2025 randomised trial found
experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with AI on their own repositories, having
expected to be faster [METR25]. Its 2026 follow-up, with 57 developers and over 800 tasks,
estimated that returning developers took 18% less time with AI (the interval runs from 38% less
to 9% more) and new ones 4% less (from 15% less to 9% more), and METR itself calls the result "only
very weak evidence" because developers increasingly refuse to work without AI and select their
tasks accordingly [METR25]. A clean measurement became impossible to run at about the moment
it became interesting.

Above the firm, the numbers shrink. Linking adoption surveys to Danish administrative records,
Humlum and Vestergaard estimate precise null effects on earnings and hours, ruling out effects
larger than 2% two years after ChatGPT's launch, even as employers reorganised tasks around it
[HV26]. Acemoglu's task-based model puts the ten-year gain in total factor productivity at no
more than 0.66%, and below 0.53% once hard-to-learn tasks are allowed for [ACE24]. US workers
told Bick, Blandin and Deming that generative AI assisted between 1% and 5% of all work hours
and saved the equivalent of 1.4% of them [BBD24]. And US nonfarm labour productivity has grown
at 2.1% a year since the end of 2019, exactly its rate since 1947 [BLS26].

The standard reading of that gap is the productivity J-curve. A general-purpose technology
needs complementary investment, mostly intangible, in reorganised processes, skills, data and
software. Spending on intangibles is poorly measured, so in the early years output looks too
low for the inputs, and later, when the intangibles pay off, it looks too high. Brynjolfsson,
Rock and Syverson estimate that adjusting for intangibles linked to computer hardware and
software puts US productivity in 2017 15.9% above the official level [BRS21]. Paul David made
the older version of the argument: the electric dynamo raised factory productivity only after
factories were rebuilt around it, decades after it was available [DAV90]. The IT boom of the
1990s showed the pattern resolving: information technology accounted for about two-thirds of
the one-point rise in productivity growth between the first and second halves of that decade
[OS00]. Comin and Hobijn put the average lag between a technology's invention and a country's
adoption at 45 years, across fifteen technologies and two centuries [CH10].

All of that is consistent with AI's gains being delayed. None of it shows they are. A J-curve
is a mechanism for why a real gain could hide, not evidence that a hidden gain exists, and a
flat productivity line is what both a delayed large effect and a prompt small one look like
in 2026. *Hypothesised*: the difference will show first not in the national figure but in the
spread between industries. If AI's gains are real and delayed, industries most exposed to it
should begin to pull away from their own pre-2023 trends before the aggregate moves; if the
gains are small, no such divergence should appear. Eloundou and colleagues supply the exposure
measure: about 80% of US workers have at least a tenth of their tasks exposed to language
models, and 19% at least half, but they make no prediction of timing [ELO23].

Two warnings about reading these series together. First, investment does not have to lead
productivity. A strong economy funds capital spending, so causality can run from growth to
investment as easily as the other way. Second, the series that rose together from 2023 to 2026
share common causes: interest rates, the demand cycle after the pandemic, and a decade of cloud
building that was under way before ChatGPT. Lines that move together in that period establish
nothing about which drives which.

## 3 The adoption problem

### Wide and shallow

"How many use AI" has several honest answers. In late 2024 nearly 40% of US adults aged 18 to
64 used generative AI, 23% of workers had used it for work in the previous week, and 9% every
working day [BBD24]. A year later the same economists' Real-Time Population Survey put work use
at about 41% and daily use at about 12% [FED26]. Firms answer differently depending on who is asked: an
employment-weighted 78% of firms in the Atlanta Fed's executive survey report using AI, while
the Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey found about 18% at the end of 2025 and
19.8% in May 2026, rising to 37% among firms with at least 250 employees [FED26] [BTOS26]. The
Census changed its question in November 2025, from AI used "in producing goods or services" to
AI used "in any business function", so its series is two pieces that do not join [BTOS26].

The gap between "uses AI at all" and "uses it every day for work" is the gap that matters for
productivity, and it is large. So is the gap between work and everything else: on ChatGPT's
consumer plans, messages that are not about work rose from 53% to more than 70% of use between
late 2022 and mid-2025 [CHA25]. Depth also differs by channel. On Claude, conversations in
which people hand the whole task to the model rose from 27% to 39% of the total in nine months,
and 77% of conversations through the programming interface showed automation patterns [AEI25].
That last figure is one vendor's traffic, but it hints at where depth lives: in software that
calls a model, not in people chatting with one.

### Three pathways

AI reaches work by at least three routes, and they have different economics.

![A two by two diagram of how AI reaches work: general-purpose or embedded, defined task or open-ended objective, shaded by the expertise the operator needs](figures/the-price-of-tomorrow/adoption-grid.svg)

The first is **conversational use**: a person asks a general-purpose model to draft, summarise,
translate or explain. It needs little expertise to start, the user checks the answer, and it
spreads like a consumer application, fast and shallow. Most adoption surveys measure this
route, which is why their headline numbers are high and their productivity numbers are low.

The second is **embedded intelligence**: a model inside a product that someone else built,
such as fraud checks, transcription, search ranking, or code completion inside an editor. The
user benefits without any AI expertise; the expertise sits with the builder. It spreads with
the product it lives in, which means at the pace of software sales and replacement, and its
value is easy to miss because nobody reports "using AI" when their spam filter improves.

The third is **expert-led open-ended work**: an operator points capable agents at a goal rather
than a task and judges what comes back. It needs the most expertise, and its value depends
almost entirely on the operator's ability to verify. It spreads person by person.

The grid has a fourth cell, an embedded product that takes an open goal to a verified result
for someone who is not an expert. It is mostly unbuilt. *Hypothesised*: that cell is where the
distance between demonstrated capability and captured value is widest, and where the
industry's revenue projections implicitly place most of their growth. If it fills, the
optimistic trajectories below become likely; if it stays empty, they do not.

### A worked example of the third pathway

Harbour, where this essay is written, is an example of the third pathway, and it is offered as
a case study, not as evidence about the economy. Harbour is a control plane that one
experienced engineer, John Kershaw, uses to send AI coding agents to work on tickets in an
issue tracker, review their output with other agents, and count a change as done only when it
has merged and passed review. Its archive holds papers measuring its own record. It has an
expert operator, specialised workflows and particular cost limits, so it says something about
what the third pathway can do and very little about how common it will become.

Two questions from the wider argument can be put to its record honestly, using only papers it
has already published.

**Does advanced AI raise the amount of valuable work, or only the amount of work?** Over sixty
days to 12 September 2026, Harbour's tracker gained 1,484 tickets and closed 701: 2.1 created
for every one closed, most of them generated by the agents' own reviews and close-out passes,
and 52% of generated tickets were never worked [H-TGT]. A companion paper collapsed related
tickets into units and found that units which reached done caused 1.52 further units each,
while the rest caused 0.18; three in four units caused nothing [H-RTR]. A hand reading of forty
never-worked tickets found 34 still true and 38 clearly stated, and 23 of them were the parent
ticket's own unfinished scope filed as a neighbour [H-NWP]. *Inferred*: the agents raise the
amount of work attempted a great deal; much of it is real; a minority is finished; and
finished work generates more work than it closes. Whether the *valuable* output per unit of
attempt has risen is not something these papers measured. It goes to Next as a proposal, not
to this essay as a finding.

**Is the cost of capability the cost of useful work?** Three measures are easy to run together
and must be kept apart. The first is the price of a fixed level of capability. Epoch AI found
the price of reaching a given benchmark score falling between 9 and 900 times a year depending
on the task, 40 times a year for GPT-4's level on a set of graduate science questions, over
about three years, with the fastest falls the most recent and the least certain to last [EPO25].
The second is the length of task a model can complete. METR found the task length at which
models succeed half the time doubling roughly every seven months over six years, and in its
2026 update every 89 to 197 days depending on the window; the best model in that update
succeeded half the time on tasks of about 320 minutes, with a confidence interval from 170 to 729 [METR-H].
That is software work, at a 50% success rate, with few long tasks timed against real humans.
The third is the cost of a successfully verified outcome: what it costs to get one piece of
work done, checked and accepted. **No public series measures it.** Kapoor and colleagues argue
that agents should be judged on cost and accuracy together [KAP24], and leaderboards report
snapshots, but nobody tracks the cost of a verified result over time.

Harbour's own record shows why the third measure is not the first two. In October 2026 its
paper on capacity found the models had moved past the bar it had measured: a mid-priced model
now scored with the strongest one on the one independent benchmark that still separates them,
and a cheap one scored near the top of the band where every model Harbour had seen build sat,
so re-tiering could carry two to three times the work for the same money. It also recorded that
neither move had been tried on a real ticket [H-CC]. An earlier trial of cheap models found
they could implement small tickets, "and the review is the cost" [H-CI]; a literature paper
found verification named by every source that measures it as the gate on handing agents more
autonomy [H-VER]. Over the same months, Harbour's count of correct, complete changes fell from
about 95 a week in June to about 47 from mid-July, at 26 to 49 agent sessions and 2.7 to 4.4
working hours per change [H-MT]. Its own analysis traced that fall to a change of regime, fewer
merged tickets and a lower share judged correct, not to the models [H-WTH], and its escaped-
defect count rose mostly because its agents began finding and filing older faults [H-REL]. Its
headline cost per verified task reads empty, because the price table lacks rows for the models
now doing the work [H-PROP]. *Observed, for one operator*: capability got cheaper and stronger
while the cost of verified useful work did not visibly fall, because other things decide it:
review, coordination, the work the system generates for itself, and whether anyone is
measuring. Nothing here says that is true of the economy. It says that the economy's missing
series is the one that would show it.

## 4 When the financial and technological cycles collide

A financial correction would not leave the technology where it is. Its effects run in different
directions and arrive at different times.

- **Surplus compute could make AI cheaper.** If financing stops before the buildings are full,
  the capacity remains and its price falls. The telecom bust left fibre in the ground that later
  carried the internet's growth [CHW03]. *Hypothesised*: on this channel a crash lowers the cost
  of capability within a year or two.
- **Funding could dry up for frontier work and start-ups.** Training runs and young companies
  depend on new capital, not on existing capacity. This channel slows capability, with a lag of
  a model generation.
- **Providers could raise prices.** Today's prices sit beneath projected losses [OAI26b]. A
  provider that must turn cash-positive will charge more, and a workflow that is economic at a
  subsidised price may not be at a sustainable one. This is the channel that reaches the cost of
  verified work most directly, and it pulls against the first.
- **Consolidation would reduce competition.** Fewer providers capture more of the value, which
  helps the survivors' returns and hurts users' surplus.
- **More efficient small models could spread capability anyway.** Prices for a fixed capability
  have fallen fastest at the bottom of the range [EPO25]; a cheap model that clears the bar for
  a defined task does not need a solvent frontier lab.
- **A provider in distress could strand what is built on it.** Products, integrations and
  practices built around one model's behaviour do not move freely; the third pathway is most
  exposed, because its workflows are tuned to particular tools.

These effects make a single verdict unlikely, and they shape the four trajectories John asked to
be weighed. They are competing hypotheses, each with an observable signature, not predictions.

![Illustration, not a published series: four small panels showing what each hypothesis predicts for valuations, use and supplier returns from 2023 to 2030](figures/the-price-of-tomorrow/four-trajectories.svg)

**Dot-com.** Valuations and capital spending collapse; use keeps spreading on the cheap capacity
left behind. Its signature is falling rental prices for compute, rising use, and losses
concentrated among lenders and late investors. It fits a world where the first effect above
dominates. It is the telecom story [CHW03] and the most common historical pattern in the BIS's
list [BIS26].

**Productivity breakthrough.** Returns arrive fast enough to justify much of the spending:
revenue at the frontier grows roughly as projected, the embedded open-ended cell fills, and
exposed industries pull away from their trends. Its signature is provider margins widening while
prices per verified outcome fall, which would mean the supply side is capturing a share of a
growing surplus.

**AI winter.** Poor returns cut research, infrastructure and adoption together. Its signature is
falling use alongside falling investment, and rising prices for the use that remains. The
evidence so far is weakest for this trajectory: every adoption series available rose through
2025 and the first half of 2026 [FED26] [BTOS26], and the Bank of England recorded the sharp
July 2026 fall in AI and semiconductor stocks as orderly, with no spillover to core markets
[BOE26]. But a winter would begin with exactly that kind of tremor, and whether use held
through it is something the Census survey can now show.

**Divided economy.** The technology delivers real value, mostly to its users, while suppliers
struggle to earn a return. Its signature is rising consumer surplus, rising use and falling
prices together with thin or negative supplier margins: the shape of Nordhaus's base rate
[NOR04] and of the 2026 surplus estimate [BCE26]. Of the four, it is the one that requires no
break from what is already observed.

*Inferred*: the record to October 2026 sits closest to the divided economy. *Hypothesised*:
if a correction comes, it starts in the financing layer and looks like the dot-com case,
because that is where the debt is and where the capacity would outlast it. That is a reading,
not a forecast, and every part of it can be overturned by the indicators in section 5.

### Are the financial and diffusion timelines separable?

The brief assumed two clocks: a fast financial one and a slow diffusion one. It asked whether
they are really separate. The evidence says they are coupled at one end and not the other.

At the **supply edge** they are tightly coupled. Microsoft says demand exceeds capacity
[MSFT26]; OpenAI's revenue grew in step with the gigawatts it could run [OAI26a]; and the price
a user pays is set by a provider's need to service its financing [OAI26b] [BOE26]. A funding
shock therefore reaches users directly, through availability and price, and it reaches them
within a year. The coupling is strongest for the third pathway and for embedded products that
call frontier models, because both buy compute by the token.

At the **organisational edge** they look separable. Adoption depth is limited by what
organisations have learned to do, not by what they can buy. Daily use at 12% against 41% any
use [FED26], null effects on earnings and hours two years in [HV26], and assisted hours of 1–5%
[BBD24] all describe a constraint that cheaper or dearer compute would hardly move. The
buildings themselves outlast the financing, as fibre did.

*Hypothesised*: the two clocks are coupled through the price and supply of compute and largely
separable through organisational learning. The decisive test is whether adoption depth keeps
rising through a funding shock. The 2026 sell-offs are small natural experiments; the
Census's biweekly survey runs straight through them and, on its new basis, can be read before
and after each one [BTOS26]. *Predicted*: if depth dips after a funding shock, the clocks are
more coupled than this reading allows; if it does not, the separable half holds.

## Next

The essay leaves three questions that evidence could answer, and puts them in `proposals.md`.

- **What does a verified outcome cost, and is it falling?** No public series measures the cost
  of getting a piece of work done, checked and accepted, though it is the quantity the economy is
  buying. Lighthouse could build one: a fixed basket of verifiable tasks with hidden acceptance
  tests, re-run each quarter on the then-current models at then-current prices, reporting cost
  per accepted result at a fixed success rate. Kept independent of Harbour, it would separate
  the price of capability from the price of useful work through 2027–2030 and tell the
  trajectories in section 4 apart on the axis that matters most.
- **Do AI-exposed industries pull away from their own trends?** The cleanest test of delayed
  against small gains. Pair BLS industry productivity with an exposure measure and track each
  industry against its pre-2023 trend, quarterly; a Lighthouse series, not a Harbour one.
- **Does Harbour's agent fleet raise valuable work, or only work?** Its papers show more work
  attempted, a minority finished, and finished work generating more than it closes, but never
  measured value per attempt. Join the throughput measure (changes that merged, passed review
  and survived 30 days) to the cost record, monthly since August 2026, once the missing price
  rows exist, and report correct, complete changes per API-equivalent dollar and the share of
  generated tickets that reach that bar.

The remaining indicators in section 5 can be tracked from published sources without a new
paper. This essay's version 1 is not independently checked; a check of its figures against
their sources would be welcome, and section 4's separability reading is the claim most worth
attacking.
