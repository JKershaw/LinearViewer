# Every Choice Was Right at the Time

*Good choices, built on past the end of their reasons*

Prepared for John Kershaw | November 2026

John Kershaw, who owns Harbour, put it this way on 14 November: every choice was right at the time, and the mistake was to keep building on a choice after it had outlived its intention. The record bears most of this out. Harbour's mess is better read as good choices built on past the end of their reasons than as bad choices, and that shape recurs at every scale, up to the loop of agents meant to clear it. It also separates two acts: tidying inside a choice has bought percentages and often added code, while the large removals on record each took a choice away, and each retirement this essay traced to its start began with a person's ruling. But the record falls short of the framing in places. Some choices were wrong the day they were made. Nothing shows the clearing is easy. The hundredfold John imagines for the front end is unmeasured; the largest gain this essay found on record is about thirteen to one, on one text. The loop sends the person clauses when it should send choices. And the loop built to clear the debt ran up the same debt inside two weeks; turning it on itself is still a plan.

Harbour is a web app that reads a team's issue tracker, writes a prompt for an AI coding agent from each ticket, sends the agent off and checks what comes back. Agents built almost all of it, and Harbour's own tickets go through the same pipeline [H-method].

## One day in November

The day began with a stall. Harbour runs work in lanes, each a run of related tickets in one area of the code driven by one supervising agent session. Overnight the lanes had stopped, waiting on rulings (a ruling is a question put to John as a card with a suggested answer), and the questions were not his kind. One came from a measurement written into a ticket's Done-when, its finish condition, so a reading meant to inform had become a gate. The area notes, a prose file per area of the code where agents record lessons and a "Waiting" list of known work, had grown from about 11 KB to 89 KB in six days and were acting as a second backlog. John: "a rule added by an agent, becomes a rule that blocks or causes more work to be done unnecessarily." [J] [LOG]

A fresh read of the history of the steering text, the prompts, rules and notes agents read before they work, found additions outnumbering removals about six to one. John: "How do we get out of Groundhog Day?" The survey session, the long-running agent session that drives this work with John and keeps a log, answered with removal and, in the same reply, proposed three new rules. John: "You propose the only solution is removing things. But then suggested rules, additional complexity, and giving agents authority to change the core parts. I think we're seeing the issue." Then: "extra scaffolding and temporary structures are fine. We just need to plan their removal." [J]

From there the talk widened. The notes might be better kept as checked data than as prose: "simply saving the notes as TS, so they get checks, and can have a reliable, duplicate free, easy to use data structure." Everything Harbour holds might need the same care: "code, comments, docs, notes, papers, tasks, logs... how do these (and others) relate, move, and need to be held in a shape." And on tangled code: "if everything is in its right place, no tangles. And, likely, much much simpler. Nothing fights its shape." [J]

The day produced three papers ([steering text](steering-text.md) [H-steer], [beyond the code](beyond-the-code.md) [H-beyond], [lanes across the seam](lanes-across-the-seam.md) [H-lanes]). Then John said three things [J]:

> "I imagine Harbour looks at the code, maps it out, then figures out what's holding complexity or mess simply because a square peg is trying to go in a round hole. Eg in hindsight our UI could be 100x simpler, and get the same results. The right shape for the right choices, and that's the Theseus method in full effect."

> "I assume every choice was right at the time. The mistake was continuing to build on it when it had outlived it's original intention. But, prior to the past few weeks, that wasn't a natural part of Harbour's process. Now we understand how it happens, and for the first time we have a mechanism to clear that tech debt easily. It requires humans to rule (eg switching to a react front end, or htnx, or ejs), but that's exactly right."

> "That's also the exact pattern we've built in our loop. It's fractal."

The [Theseus method](theseus-method.md) is Harbour's way of renewing its code while it runs: find each decision scattered across the code, gather it into the area that owns it, then rebuild it from a fresh design [H-method].

## Right at the time

"I assume" is the right word, because it changes the question. "What is wrong with this code?" invites a list. "What was this for, and is that still true?" has an answer, and the record usually holds it.

The idea is as old as the debt metaphor. Ward Cunningham, who coined it in 1992, named the pitfall "the failure to consolidate" ([C92](http://c2.com/doc/oopsla92.html)), and later said what he had not meant: "I'm never in favor of writing code poorly, but I am in favor of writing code to reflect your current understanding of a problem even if that understanding is partial" ([C09](https://c2.com/wiki/remodel/pages/WardExplainsDebtMetaphor)). In its first sense, debt is yesterday's understanding still standing. Martin Fowler, on eBay: "The right architecture to support 1996-ebay isn't going to be the right architecture for 2006-ebay" ([F14](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/SacrificialArchitecture.html)).

Harbour's front end is the worked case. Its first commit, on 4 January 2026, was a read-only viewer of one issue tracker's projects as a collapsible tree: twelve files, 912 lines of code outside the lock file, one browser script of 188 lines. Its instruction file for agents, `CLAUDE.md`, carried one line among the design principles: "Keep it minimal - no frameworks, no build step" [H-first]. No reason was written beside it. None was needed.

Other choices have their reasons on record. The code that reads issue trackers took Linear's wire format as its shape when Linear was the only tracker [H-theseus]; prompt sentences went in where something had failed [H-steer].

"Every" is too strong, though. Some choices were wrong when made. On 8 November Harbour built a settings switch from a brief that had turned a passing answer of John's into a product mechanism; meeting it, John said: "technically it's correct, but it's also obviously wrong and a mistake." The paired area read, a blind before-and-after reading of a sample of an area's files, was written into two lanes' finish conditions, and the session's own review later found: "The kit had no power to detect a change, so making it a gate guaranteed a ruling." [LOG] A consolidation in October put a shared helper in another area and so re-tied a two-way dependency between areas five days after a deletion had untied it [H-theseus].

So "every" is a stance, not a finding, but a useful one. G. K. Chesterton's reformer, finding a fence across a road, says "I don't see the use of this; let us clear it away," and is told to "Go away and think." When he can say what the use was, "I may allow you to destroy it" ([CH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._K._Chesterton)). The assumption sends you to find the use. John's framing adds the step Chesterton leaves out: asking whether the use has ended.

## Building on it

On 14 November the front end had 44 browser scripts and 35,236 lines, about 187 times the founding script [H-first].

Much of it stands because of the founding line, each piece a sensible answer given the line. Research in June called the hand-written components, built from template strings, "the deliberate, build-step-free answer to 'are we inventing our own framework?'" ([H-482](../../lin-482-ui-simplification-research.md)). Reading the rule as "no shared module between server and browser" produced eleven pieces of server logic copied by hand into browser scripts, and an October paper named the founding line the "largest single producer" of such twins ([H-coherence](coherence-as-it-grows.md)). The tests gained a hand-written imitation of the browser's document model, 1,039 lines, added on 5 November, with a 785-line test whose job is to make its use compulsory [H-first].

This is John's second claim, and the best supported. Once a choice is fixed, every later choice is judged against it rather than against the need it once served. Each is locally right, and the misfit compounds. The complexity sits not in the square peg or the round hole but in everything built to hold one in the other. The code that reads issue trackers (Harbour calls each adapter a provider) shows it too: "In practice the shape is Linear's GraphQL wire format, and the other providers rebuild it in their own normalisers" ([H-theseus](theseus.md)). So do the area notes: within six days five kinds of writer were appending to them, a validator and a quota policed them, and one repository's Waiting list went from 30 entries to 118 [H-beyond] [LOG].

None of this was for want of noticing. The front end's rule was questioned three times and kept each time. On 16 March a plan accepted a copy that "will be embedded/duplicated in the client script since there's no build step" ([H-swim](../../../plans/swim-view.md)). On 15 June research weighed "ejs / htmx / React" and said no; the direction "was decided with the owner", the rule "was reaffirmed *for now*", and htmx "has real merit" but was "Deferred to Step 2" [H-482]. A later review: "Step 2 was never ticketed." ([H-debrief](../../reviews/v1-passage-debrief-2026-10-10/backlog-questions.md)) On 8 October the twins paper said: "Browsers load ES modules without a build step, so the rule does not require the copies; the reading of it does." [H-coherence]

"For now" had no date and no sign to ask again, which is typical. None of 27 notable process changes since June named a condition for retiring it, and the one sunset written into a process step was never read ([H-land](how-process-changes-land.md)). The steering-text paper gives three reasons temporary structure stays: "Nothing notices that its job is done, nobody owns removing it, and sometimes nothing could remove it even if asked." [H-steer] The front end adds a fourth: its reason was never written down, so nothing could have checked it. Continuing to build is not an error someone makes. It is the default, because a choice arrives with a beginning and no end.

## Tidying inside a choice, and retiring it

"100x" makes sense once two acts are told apart: tidying inside a choice, and retiring it. The record separates them sharply.

Inside a choice, tidying has bought percentages and often added code. Of 57 commits whose titles say cut, remove, simplify, fold or retire and that touched the steering text, 37 left it larger, and trimming single rules "has never moved the total by as much as a kilobyte" [H-steer]. The October charter, a four-day programme to "simplify the code, unify the UI, and update the pages" in John's words ([H-charter](how-the-charter-got-complicated.md)), ended with production code 9,725 lines larger; byte-identical moves took one server file from 3,782 lines to 882 while the directory around it grew from 26,955 to 28,559 ([H-retro](../../reviews/charter-retro-2026-10-26/README.md)).

On the tidy lanes of 13 and 14 November the instruments disagree. Harbour's Periodicals are scheduled independent reviews of the codebase. The [Stability Review](../../reviews/stability-review-2026-11-14.md) counts the tidy programmes at "net +781 production lines" and calls the pattern "replacement churn" [H-stab]. The [Drift & Coherence review](../../reviews/drift-coherence-review-2026-11-14.md) reads the window as "better", with seven earlier findings resolved [H-drift]. The lanes' own paired reads found "no detectable difference" (D = +0.25 and −0.3, inside a ±0.5 band), from samples that mostly missed the changed files, 3 of 14 in one lane ([H-reads](../../plans/survey/area-read-reports/comparison-302edafc3-vs-4b5001ddf.md)). None of them shows the code smaller. Even careful rebuilding inside a choice gives a percentage by size: three design-first rebuilds cut code "by up to 39% in a file" ([H-ladder](rebuild-ladder.md)).

Shape is a different measure, and there the Theseus method did better. Gathering the 24 decisions scattered through the endpoints that start agent runs, then rebuilding what was gathered, took the worst function from 63 decision points to 29 to 13, and the places holding those decisions from 95 to 26. The gathering took 317 lines out of production code ([H-consolidate](consolidate-then-rebuild.md)). That is a multiple, on one function's shape, and it came the way the others did: copies were deleted.

Retiring a choice is a different order of thing. Until October a model wrote the prompt for each stage of the pipeline from a 106 KB "meta-prompt", and from June to 7 October 53 of the 63 pull requests that changed the stage definitions (84%) had to change the meta-prompt too. On 4 and 5 October that path was deleted: "the 106 KB meta-prompt is gone", "and an 8 KB selector took its place" [H-coherence]. The first deletion took a net 1,012 lines out of 27 application files ([H-paths](one-job-many-paths.md)). Deleting the thing a passage described accounts for 74% of all the real shrinkage in the steering text that agents carry on every run [H-steer]. The charter's clearest win was a deletion too: one superseded page removed, 7,681 lines [H-retro]. Two-way dependencies between areas, which Harbour calls tangles, tell the same story: 24 born from January to mid-October, 5 dissolved, and "All 5 dissolutions took something away." Six of the 24 births came from consolidations [H-theseus].

What these share is that something was taken away, and retiring a choice takes the most, because what was built to serve it goes with it. So the kind of gain John names is in the record. Its size is not. The largest this essay found is about thirteen to one, 106 KB to 8 KB on one text. Nothing comes near a hundred, and for the front end nothing has been measured. The 187 is how far the code has grown, not how much could go: the product now has more than a dozen named views ([H-views](../../architecture/views.md)).

Nor has the record blamed the front end's platform: in June it blamed adoption of the component layer, in October the reading of the rule. On 13 November, the day before the quotations above, John wrote that "the UI issues trace back to one of my very first rules: no build step… it's the biggest target of the Theseus method." Yet the ticket keeps the rule: "There's still no build step and no framework: the rule stays, read as allowing the browser to import, as browsers do natively" ([H-4446](lanes-across-the-seam-evidence/issues/LIN-4446.json)). Three pages became native modules the same day [H-first]. The record's best present reading is that the front end's square peg was a reading of a rule. Whether another platform would be a hundred times simpler is open: nobody has built one page both ways.

## Not yet a habit

The record agrees that retiring was not routine. About six process changes added a step, a rule or a scheduled wake-up for every one that removed one [H-land]; 6.3 bytes of steering text went in for every byte that came out [H-steer]; no tangle dissolved between 20 January and 6 October [H-theseus]. The retirements that did happen were events, each begun by a person from outside the ordinary flow. John re-scoped the meta-prompt's ticket twice on 4 October, and a long-running operator session did the work directly rather than through the pipeline [H-3300]. Share links, whose removal dissolved two tangles, went because they "aren't what John wants"; the charter's page went on John's word that it "has probably been superseded" [H-removals].

What is new is the looking. The Theseus method made rebuilding a routine with a judge in mid-October [H-method], and since 8 November code sends every fresh ticket through a look-first sequence before any building ([H-survey](../../plans/survey.md)). That round, look first, size the move, build, tidy what is left, is what John calls the loop. "For the first time" is fair for the looking, not for the removing.

## Who rules

Each retirement this essay traced to its start began with a ruling: the meta-prompt, share links, the charter's page, the front end's rule re-read. Small ones too. The settings switch went about three quarters of an hour after John's call, leaving its area 137 lines smaller; the paired reads' fixed sample went on a word from John, which the log gives as "John: drop it." [LOG]

The Theseus method asks people "for very little", and can settle ownership by rule, because its moves leave behaviour unchanged [H-method]. Retiring a choice changes what the thing is for, which is not written in the code, so it needs a person. An agent will not do it. The survey session's fresh review: "Text gets its authority from where it sits, and the only way out of it is John." [LOG] To an agent, a standing choice reads as a constraint. And asked to remove, agents add: the session's three new rules, and the paper that found six additions for every removal, whose own recommendation became a new rule [LOG].

Here the record cuts against the framing: as built, the loop has its questions the wrong way round. In the two tidy lanes the survey log records fifteen rulings in about a day and, by this essay's count of its own labels, marks about ten as questions the lane could have settled, such as engineering trade-offs and clauses an agent had written. Both lanes sat idle overnight on answers. Meanwhile the choice to take a larger move goes ahead under a standing policy, "err larger", with no ruling [H-survey]. Of 45 rulings withdrawn when their ticket closed, about 15 "were scope or design calls the run settled itself", a few of them small product calls that shipped: "What was lost was John's say, not the information." [LOG] Clauses reach the person; choices do not.

A person's rulings outlive their reasons too. The front end's founding rule was John's. In October a comment of his that prototypes "may be over specific" became a policy, "No prototypes, screenshots or design review pages go to builders", which the charter's retro named as the cause of pages that did not match their designs [H-retro]. Since 7 November Harbour has sealed every filed ticket description as the person's words, including text that planners and other agents drafted [LOG]. A ruling needs what any choice needs: its reason kept with it, and a way to mark it over. Michael Nygard's decision records keep a Context and a Status that can become "superseded", so that "The motivation behind previous decisions is visible for everyone, present and future" ([N11](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions)).

## The same shape at every size

For the debt, John's "fractal" holds in full.

| Scale | The choice | What stayed or was built on it |
|---|---|---|
| A line | A marker kept "until LIN-4015 retires the tone names" | The ticket is Done; the marker is still there [H-steer] |
| An area | Linear's wire format as the shape of an issue | Four other providers rebuild it; three helpers translate back [H-theseus] |
| The platform | "no frameworks, no build step" | Components, hand-made copies, an imitation document model [H-first] [H-coherence] |
| The process | A measurement written as a finish condition | A gate, then rulings, then idle lanes [LOG] |
| The session | Asked how to get out, it answered with removal | Three new rules in the same reply [LOG] |
| The person | A comment made for a reason | A policy applied past it [H-retro] |

It repeats because one rule makes it at every size. Whoever acts next, an agent on a ticket, a session running lanes or a person answering a card, meets what is there as given, answers the local problem by adding, and has neither the reason nor the standing to take anything away. The steering-text paper gathers outside evidence that people overlook subtraction [H-steer].

The cure partly shares the shape. Before a task starts, the Survey, the loop's first step, asks "why is this difficult?" and offers moves in sizes, from "Just drill" to "Theseus the area. Free the seam, consolidate, rebuild." [H-survey] The Theseus method's first moves, Find and Own, ask it of the codebase [H-method]; John's picture, of the whole.

The last level is not shown. The loop's own machinery ran up the debt in under two weeks. Its plan had warned: "A new step is a new gate, and gates are where prose collects." [H-survey] On 12 November the blind read, a fresh reader scoring a change from its code alone, became a gate in code that a ticket cannot close without [H-survey], a new floor of about $1.02 on every task [H-stab]. Some of this went within the hour of John's word. Most still stands as this is written: the notes' quota, the blind-read gate, and the cut rule, which since 7 November has sent any narrowing of a ticket to John [H-survey]. The agreed next step is to take them one at a time, "stays, goes, or stays with a planned removal, in John's words" [LOG]. That would be the loop turned on itself. It is a plan.

## What it asks for

In practice the framing asks for little that is new: a standing choice kept with its reason and the sign that it is over; a looking step that asks about the choices a task stands on, not only the code; and the choice, not the clause, put to the person.

Where the framing runs ahead of the record:

- **"Every."** Some choices were wrong when made.
- **"Easily."** Quick to carry out once ruled, but not shown easy to find or to prove. The largest rebuild is "about 930 lines over two files" [H-ladder], and the Theseus method's own test, later changes landing smaller, "has not had time to show" [H-method].
- **"100x."** Unmeasured. The largest this essay found on record is about thirteen to one, on one text.
- **"It requires humans to rule."** True of every retirement traced to its start, and as built the loop asks the person the wrong questions.
- **The evidence.** One codebase, one owner, a few weeks; and much of the loop's evidence is the survey session's own log about itself.
