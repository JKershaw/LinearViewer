# Tasks are tasks, and the codebase is a place

Coding agents make good contractors: given a work order, they do the job, usually well, and leave. But a codebase is a place, and nobody lives in it. The agent that notices a loose stair is held to its ticket, so it notes the stair and walks on. The next agent starts fresh and finds the stair again, because what the first one learned was filed under the job, not the place. Every task is looked after. Nobody's job is the place.

This essay comes from a day spent reading the record of Harbour, a system that runs coding agents over a team's tickets. A ticket is a work item in the tracker. Harbour hands each one to a series of stages, each a fresh AI session that reads the ticket and does one job, such as research, planning, plan-review (a second reader that checks the plan before anyone builds), building or review [3]. What follows draws on [the investigation's notes](https://github.com/JKershaw/LinearViewer/blob/bb20e5f7/docs/reviews/_evidence-2026-11-02-ticket-intent/investigation-notes.md), which hold every count, quote and ticket behind it; every figure and quote below is from them unless it carries a marker to another source. It also draws on three earlier papers that studied the code itself, and on the record of what Harbour has tried before; those figures carry markers. The cases are readings, not measurements, and the notes say so too. John Kershaw, the engineer who runs Harbour, gave the investigation its image.

## Nobody's job is the place

A contractor and a resident see the same house differently. The contractor is there for the work order, and doing that well means not wandering off to do other work. The resident walks past the squeaky stair every day and one morning fixes it on the way. Harbour's agents are contractors, and good ones. What the codebase lacks is a resident, and three things follow from that. Together they are the argument.

**The one who feels the friction is held to the ticket.** An agent building a feature meets the code as it is: the rule kept in three places, the override stacked on an override, the file that does too many jobs. It notices, and it says so. In roughly three-quarters of 256 tickets' comment threads, agents record friction they saw and left alone, in words like "pre-existing" and "out of scope, note only". The agents' write-ups even have a standing field for it, "improvement noticed, not required", which appears 158 times. Then the agent leaves the friction where it is, because the brief tells it to. One agent put the whole pattern into two sentences: widening the work "would be the scope creep the brief forbids. Recording as a finding for LIN-2149's owner to ticket, not filing it myself".

That is good contractor behaviour. Scope rules exist for real reasons: an agent that wanders off its ticket is harder to review and easier to get wrong. But the rule puts the one who knows most about the stair, the one standing on it, in the one position from which it cannot be fixed. The note goes to an owner, and the place has none. Nobody whose job it is will read the note on the way past, because nobody passes that way except the next contractor, who is also held to a ticket.

**What is learned is filed under the job.** Each stage starts cold. It is a fresh session that reads the ticket, does its job and ends [3]. So whatever an agent learns about a corner of the code goes where the next agent will look, which is the ticket, and the ticket is about the job. Even the research run that came just before these notes left its findings only on its ticket: no document landed in the repository or in the archive of papers this essay belongs to. When the job closes, the memory closes with it.

John names this as the one decision he would be willing to reopen: the ticket as the source of truth. It conflates three things, the record of the job, the documentation and plans, and the place's memory. A resident keeps the place's memory in the place. A contractor keeps it in the folder for the job.

The interface shows what that costs: one lesson about how to brief a design had to be learned twice. In late June a theme was reverted, "built without the attached design spec"; its redo shipped a re-skin, and in July a further ticket had to say "build to the mockup design (not the migration re-skin)". Build to the design, not to a survey of what is already there. In October it was learned again. John called a prototype "the benchmark for UI UX standard I want for Harbour", and also said that code or screenshots handed to a builder "may be over specific"; a ticket wrote down "No prototypes, screenshots or design review pages go to builders. Tickets carry the design in words", and the notes found no sign-off from John on that line. A later review found briefs written from surveys of the old pages and polish rounds tidying the wrong layout. The summer's lesson was on the summer's tickets, closed. October's agents read October's.

**Nobody owns the first half.** Kent Beck's advice is to "make the change easy (warning: this may be hard), then make the easy change" [2]. Inside a ticket, Harbour does only the second half. The first half, tidying the place so the next change lands clean, belongs to no stage, because no stage's job is the place. John's own version has two layers: "we want X, ah it's messy, let's tidy up first, then it lands clean", set against tickets walking the stages in a line. Tidying, for him, is small, smart, isolated steps that each leave the code simpler.

The record shows the first half happens only when it is the job. Since 1 September, 965 commits have touched the app's code, and it has grown by about 78,900 lines. About 100 of those commits shrink it, and nearly all come from tickets whose job is to retire, remove, consolidate or rebuild something. Ordinary feature and fix tickets almost never shrink code. Tidying happens when someone files it as a work order. The only ones placed to notice that it needs filing are the agents who saw the friction and were told to leave it.

Put together: the friction is felt by someone who may not fix it, written where nobody who lives there will read it, and the tidying that would remove it waits for a ticket nobody is placed to file. None of this is a fault in any agent. Each did its job. That is the point.

## The house from the other side

That is the house seen from the ticket. Three of the archive's papers look from the other side, the code itself, and tell the same story. Foote and Yoder named the shape in 1997: "PIECEMEAL GROWTH gradually allows elements of the system to sprawl in an uncontrolled fashion" [14].

**It only grows, and fastest at the gates.** Since June the stage prompts have grown five- to sixfold, and the growth collects at the gates, the steps that decide whether work passes. When something went wrong, the fix was usually a sentence: eight of the ten tickets in the close-out step's lineage changed no running code. Almost nothing leaves: of 101 ticket citations written into the prompt text, five ever left it [5]. The house gains notices on the wall.

**What grows is the number of places holding one decision.** The code did not get messier in the obvious way: duplicated text fell from 2.5% of production lines to 1.2% while the code grew four and a half times. What grew is the number of places holding one small decision: a tolerant timestamp parse went from one file to eighteen [6]. That is the loose stair at scale, fixed in one place and loose in seventeen others. It costs: 86 of 190 escaped defects (45%) trace to one decision held in more than one place, most often a sibling left behind or a change that landed in fewer places than it needed. That share is partly a measure of looking; among faults found by use rather than by a later sweep it is 30 of 110 (27%) [6]. Of Harbour's ways of paying this down, a review that files tickets had filed tickets nobody worked, and helpers half-finished. In the paper's words, "Deletion finishes" [6].

**Pointed at the place, agents look after it.** On 17 October someone pointed agents at the place itself: eight tickets: seven to free, untie, consolidate or rebuild one tangle, and one to make room for more runs at once. All eight merged, each within two and a half hours, and every production change replayed against the old code with zero behaviour differences. At the dispatch entrance, the places holding its shared decisions went from 95 to 26. John's cost was "two presses on ruling cards" [7]: he was asked to decide twice.

Read together: pointed at the place, agents look after it well and cheaply. What is missing is someone with the standing to point, day after day, at what to tidy next.

## Three moments

**The stray blue and the brand.** Reviewers can be thorough about the ticket and blind to the place. One design ticket asked that "ready is the brand". It was built exactly as written: any row with a guessed next step turned teal. Four days later a round of feedback said "the colour that should say 'this one' says nothing", and the guessed steps were taken out. Before that, the ticket had been through five plan-reviews and gathered about 86,000 characters of comments. "Blue" appears about 120 times in that record, as reviewers hunted stray blue across 13 stylesheets. The ready rule appears five times, always restated as spec and never questioned. Every reviewer did the job in front of them, and the job was the ticket. Whether the rule served the screen was the place's question, and nobody was there to ask it.

**The monospace body.** The body text stayed monospace from a June decision until 26 October. About 88 local overrides grew up around it, each one a fix in a single spot, and 90 remain on main. One code comment reads "the body is monospace… without it the copy-first key note… leaked into mono". A resident would have changed the body once.

**The spiral.** Some tickets go round plan-review again and again. Narrow tickets landed fast even in the most tangled files: two narrow, line-cited, highly prescriptive tickets took about half an hour each, with no plan-review at all. Broad clean-ups did not spiral either; they took two plan-reviews or none. Broad design asks did: the brand ticket and one other hit five each. The shape repeats. Plan-review asks whether a class of things is closed. The thing is scattered through the code, so each round finds another member. The plan swells, the brand ticket's to "40k, 13 sheets, 15 test files", and John is asked for a ruling. The notes hold this as a hunch, and so does this essay: a spiral needs tangled code, a broad ask that cuts across it, and a goal that is a judgement rather than a closed check. If the hunch holds, the tangle is the place's condition showing up as a ticket's cost, and the cheapest fix is the one no ticket is for.

## Harbour has tried before

None of this is new to John: "I knew I'd already tried this... dozens of times" [12]. Most tries left no record. Three did, and each gave the place a voice without a resident.

**Inspectors.** Harbour has sixteen periodicals, standing reviews such as Drift & Coherence, Seams & Tangles, Comprehension-Debt and Design & Interface, John's first answer to "how do we look after the growing pile of code?" They run on a cadence, and the agent that keeps Harbour busy takes an overdue one before the top of the queue unless a person has set a goal [8]. But they visit on a schedule, from outside any task's timeline: they write a report, mint a few tickets into the same queue as every other work order, and leave. An inspector can say the stair is loose. It does not live there.

**A budget.** Passages, Harbour's plans for a stretch of work, reserved a maintenance leg, one strand of the plan: whenever an inspector was due, that leg made room for it, from the same pool as every other leg [9]. It reserved room to run the inspectors, whose output is more tickets, not anyone with standing to decide what to tidy. John's verdict is that it missed the point and never really worked [12]; the record holds no measure of its outcome.

**A question turned to serve the ticket.** In June, research was asked to end with a Surface Assessment: "would a refactor make this land better?" That is close to the resident's question. Within a week it misfired, producing a blocking refactor for a seam that, in its own ticket's words, had "no feature values consumed yet". The follow-up called it push versus pull: a general prompt to tidy has no stopping point, and the refactor worth doing is the one the feature demands. The question became "is the feature's shape demanding a structural change?", a refactor must now name the line in this task that calls it, and a third verdict was added, "improvement noticed, not required" [10], the field that appears 158 times above. The June misfire added structure; the October work that succeeded removed it.

**What worked was someone pointing.** The Drift & Coherence inspector minted three fix tickets between June and August; on 8 October they still sat in Backlog with no comments [6]. On 11 October John started a coherence loop: a session ran the inspector, waited for John's go, pointed agents at small tickets, and ran the inspector again. All three tickets were done that day, and the next reading found the import cycles inside `lib/` gone and 332 fewer production lines [11]. The loop's last log entry is from 19 October, and on 23 October it was parked; its parking note handed the work to a charter John steered, which turned to the interface within two days and closed on 26 October. That day's edition counted 54 decisions held in more than one place, against 43 four days earlier, though the edition says most of the 12 new ones were born in a wider window [11]. During the loop the count rose too, though mostly because a new census first saw decisions that were already there. The bursts fixed what they were pointed at, and the rest of the place kept accruing.

Both bursts that worked, the coherence loop from 11 October and the separate day of untangling on 17 October, were a person pointing agents at the place. Neither left anyone whose job was to keep pointing.

## How we found this

The investigation set out to find where a request loses its intent on the way through Harbour, and expected a leaky stage. It found none. In 611 of 653 tickets with a snapshot from before their first stage, at least 95% of the request's sentences survive word for word, and a coverage check found about 95% of 1,856 asks delivered as written; a second rater agreed on 96.5% of labels, but the figures are rough, good to about ±50%. The check could see only the ticket's words, and two cases it counted as narrowing were good judgement: a builder who caught a self-contradicting spec and said why it deviated, and agents who followed John's steer over a spec line. The investigation kept swapping a measurement in for each question of judgement. The shape appeared only when it read the cases, and John gave it the image.

## Where the image strains

- **Some contractors have judgement.** The two cases above are agents reading through a spec to what John meant. A contractor who does that is already part resident.
- **Some spirals have nothing to do with tangle.** One ticket went three rounds of plan-review over the reading of a single house rule, on a file that had just been rebuilt.
- **Tidying as the job is not always enough.** Some tickets whose job was to reconcile two parts of the code still added lines.
- **The investigation repeated the trap it describes,** and so did this essay's first version. Its review checked it against its plan, scope and format and found it sound. A fresh reader, given it as its audience would be, came away calling it "an investigation report that ends in a reframing" [4].
- **The house is not empty.** 965 commits touched it in about two months. What is missing is not traffic. It is someone whose job, day to day, is the place's condition.

## What a resident would be

The notes leave open who would look after the place, and so does this essay. They name four candidates, and each would have to do something a work order does not.

- **A person**, with standing over the place's condition across tickets rather than within one. John's reopened decision points this way. Nobody holds that standing yet.
- **An agent** with a continuity a fresh session lacks. Each stage starts cold, so the lessons would have to live somewhere it reads [3].
- **A memory kept with the code**, so the lesson sits where the friction lives rather than on a closed ticket. Someone still has to read it and act on it.
- **A permission** to change the place outside a ticket. One case points there: a size rule meant for prompt text bounded a new kind of surface, a public setup guide, and John's reading was that the right call was for an agent to ask him whether to go past the rule.

John's current hunch is a fifth.

- **A step in the pipeline** that answers to the place and to the person's intent, not to the ticket. It would draw on the inspectors' reports or its own judgement, ask John for a ruling where the intent is in question, and count tidying what the task touches as part of its job, so that the place improves as Harbour works. It is a hunch for exploring; nothing in Harbour implements it [12]. Where it would sit is open: after research, it has research's map of the code; just before the first change, it reaches every ticket, since many skip research.

Among people this is the camp-site rule, "always leave the code behind in a better state than you found it", and Fowler is wary of anything that causes it friction, "such as strong CodeOwnership" [13]. Harbour's scope rule is that friction, held on purpose; the analogy is a mechanism to consider, not an estimate.

John pictures the place as fractal: the whole system at the top, a single function at the bottom, the tangles in between. The way through is bubbles popping into larger bubbles, a small tangle resolved freeing the larger one it sat in, so it cannot be done in one step [12]. It is the picture behind his October "neat seams and bubbles" and "compact (or consolidated) versus diffuse" [7]. A resident's work would be a sequence, not a sweep.

**What is different this time.** In John's words, "now we have a much more mature view on what it needs to do" [12]. There is a necessity gate a place-minded step could answer to instead of pushing [10], instruments to say whether the place is improving, and a method seen to work, eight of eight on 17 October [7].

**What could still make it fail.** The record already holds three ways.

- "Tidy what you touch" has been tried as advice. Drift & Coherence's fix for one scattered pattern was "adopt-on-next-touch, no sweep", and adoption stayed flat: the one file written fresh in that window imported the shared helpers and wrote eight raw copies beside them [6].
- A new step is a new gate, and gates are where prose collects [5].
- A step whose job is the place will find something on every task unless it answers to a need: the June misfire again, push not pull [10].

Another rule would not do it. The October rule about prototypes is a rule, and "ready is the brand" was a rule obeyed exactly while the screen drifted. Rules are more work orders. Whether one of the five is the resident, or whether the image gives out first, is left open on purpose.
