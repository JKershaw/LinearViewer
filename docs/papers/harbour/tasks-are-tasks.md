# Tasks are tasks, and the codebase is a place

Coding agents make good contractors: given a work order, they do the job, usually well, and leave. But a codebase is a place, and nobody lives in it. The agent that notices a loose stair is held to its ticket, so it notes the stair and walks on. The next agent starts fresh and finds the stair again, because what the first one learned was filed under the job, not the place. Every task is looked after. Nobody's job is the place.

This essay began as an investigation into whether Harbour's stages keep what was asked of them, and found something else. Harbour is a system that runs coding agents over a team's tickets. A ticket is a work item in the tracker. Harbour hands each one to a series of stages, each a fresh AI session that reads the ticket and does one job, such as research, planning, plan-review (a second reader that checks the plan before anyone builds), building or review [1]. The essay has two movements. The first is a diagnosis, drawn from [the investigation's notes](https://github.com/JKershaw/LinearViewer/blob/bb20e5f7/docs/reviews/_evidence-2026-11-02-ticket-intent/investigation-notes.md) [2] and from earlier papers on the code itself; a figure without a numbered marker is from the notes, whose cases are readings, not measurements. The second is the shape of an answer, mostly John Kershaw's, the engineer who runs Harbour [3], partly the reading of a conversation he had with Claude on Fable 5.1, an AI model, and marked as that [4].

## Nobody's job is the place

A contractor and a resident see the same house differently. The contractor is there for the work order, and doing that well means not wandering off to do other work. The resident walks past the squeaky stair daily and one morning fixes it. Harbour's agents are good contractors, and three things follow from having no resident.

**The one who feels the friction is held to the ticket.** In roughly three-quarters of the 256 comment threads a loose search turned up, agents record friction they saw and left alone, in words like "pre-existing" and "out of scope, note only". Their write-ups have a standing field for it, "improvement noticed, not required", which appears 158 times. One agent put the pattern in two sentences: widening the work "would be the scope creep the brief forbids. Recording as a finding for LIN-2149's owner to ticket, not filing it myself". That is good contractor behaviour; an agent off its ticket is harder to review. But the rule puts the one who knows most about the stair, the one standing on it, in the one position from which it may not fix it, unless the stair is the cause of its own ticket. The note goes to an owner, and the place has none.

**What is learned is filed under the job.** Each stage starts cold [1], so whatever an agent learns about a corner of the code goes on the ticket, and the ticket is about the job. When the job closes, so does the memory. John names this as the one decision he would be willing to reopen: the ticket as the source of truth. It conflates three things: the record of the job, the plans, and the place's memory.

**Nobody owns the first half.** Kent Beck's advice is to "make the change easy (warning: this may be hard), then make the easy change" [5]. Inside a ticket, Harbour does only the second half. The first half, tidying the place so the next change lands clean, belongs to no stage, because no stage's job is the place. John's version: "we want X, ah it's messy, let's tidy up first, then it lands clean", where tidying is small, isolated steps that each leave the code simpler. The record shows it happens only when it is the job. Since 1 September, 965 commits have touched the app's code, and it has grown by about 78,900 lines. About 100 of those commits shrink it, and nearly all come from tickets whose job is to retire, remove, consolidate or rebuild something.

So the friction is felt by someone who may not fix it, written where nobody who lives there will read it, and the tidying that would remove it waits for a ticket nobody is placed to file. Each agent did its job. That is the point.

### The house from the other side

The archive's papers on the code itself tell the same story from the other side.

**The rules grew, fastest at the gates.** The pipeline's own instructions are part of the house too. From June to the end of September the stage prompts grew five- to sixfold, and the growth collected at the gates, the steps that decide whether work passes. When something went wrong, the fix was usually a sentence: of the ten tickets that shaped the close-out step, the stage that finishes a ticket, eight changed no running code, and of 101 references to tickets written into the prompt text, five ever left it [6]. Since 2 October they have been held at their size [7], a point that returns below.

**What grows is the number of places holding one decision.** Duplicated text fell from 2.5% of production lines to 1.2% while the code grew four and a half times; what grew is the number of places holding one small decision. A tolerant timestamp parse went from one file to eighteen [8]: the loose stair, fixed in one place and loose in seventeen others. The Theseus method, the archive's account of renewing a codebase while it runs, names two kinds of mess [9]. Code *tangled inside*, one file of long, branching functions, is easy to see, and a rebuild from a fresh design fixes it. Code *scattered between*, one decision written in several places, looks fine file by file, and only gathering the copies into one owner fixes it. The scattered kind costs. Of 190 defects that got past review into the main branch, 86 (45%) trace to one decision held in more than one place, most often a copy left unfixed after another copy was fixed. That share is partly a measure of looking, since later sweeps went hunting for exactly these; among the defects found in use rather than by a sweep it is 30 of 110 (27%) [8].

### Four moments

**The stray blue and the brand.** One design ticket asked that "ready is the brand", that a row ready to run should wear the brand colour, and was built exactly as written: any row with a guessed next step turned teal. Four days later feedback said "the colour that should say 'this one' says nothing", and the guessed steps came out. Over five plan-reviews, "blue" appears about 120 times in its record as reviewers hunted stray, off-brand blue across 13 stylesheets; the ready rule appears five times, never questioned. Whether the rule served the screen was the place's question, and nobody was there to ask it.

**The monospace body.** The body text stayed monospace from a June decision until 26 October. About 88 local overrides grew up around it, each a fix in a single spot, and they are still there: 90 on main today. A resident would have changed the body once.

**The interface, learned twice.** In June a redesign was reverted as "built without the attached design spec"; the redo was scoped as "largely a visual migration" and shipped a re-skin, and the next ticket had to say "build to the mockup design (not the migration re-skin)". In October John called one prototype "the benchmark for UI UX standard I want for Harbour", but said code or screenshots given to a builder "may be over specific"; a relaying agent wrote "So the prototype is not attached", and a ticket made it a rule: "No prototypes, screenshots or design review pages go to builders. Tickets carry the design in words." The notes found no sign-off from John on that line. A review of the gap records what followed: briefs written from surveys of the old pages, reviews that compared each build with its ticket, polish rounds that tidied the wrong layout. June's lesson was about how to move in this one area. It was filed on June's tickets, which had closed.

**The spiral.** Narrow, line-cited tickets landed in about half an hour even in tangled files; broad design asks spiralled, two of them to five rounds each. The notes hold a hunch about why, and so does this essay: a spiral needs tangled code, a broad ask that cuts across it, and a goal that is a judgement rather than a closed check. Plan-review asks whether a class of things is closed. If the thing is scattered through the code, each round may find another member; the brand ticket's plan grew to "40k, 13 sheets, 15 test files" before John was asked for a ruling. If the hunch holds, the tangle is the place's condition showing up as a ticket's cost.

### Harbour has tried before

None of this is new to John: "I knew I'd already tried this... dozens of times" [10]. Most tries left no record. Four did, and each gave the place a voice without a resident.

**Inspectors.** Harbour has sixteen periodicals, standing reviews such as Drift & Coherence and Seams & Tangles [11], John's first answer to "how do we look after the growing pile of code?" [10]. They visit from outside any task: they write a report, mint a few tickets into the same queue as every other work order, and leave. An inspector can say the stair is loose; it does not live there.

**A budget.** Passages, Harbour's plans for a stretch of work, reserved a maintenance leg that made room for an inspector whenever one was due [12]. It reserved room for the inspectors, whose output is more tickets, not for anyone with standing to decide what to tidy. John's verdict is that it missed the point and never really worked [10].

**A question turned to serve the ticket.** In June, research was asked to end with a Surface Assessment: "would a refactor make this land better?" That is close to the resident's question. Within a week it misfired, producing a blocking refactor for a seam that had "no feature values consumed yet". The follow-up called it push versus pull: the refactor worth doing is the one the feature demands. A refactor must now name the line in this task that calls it, and a third verdict was added, "improvement noticed, not required" [13], the field that appears 158 times above.

**Authority.** On 3 October an earlier essay made this essay's diagnosis a month ahead of it: Harbour's stages "keep seeing the real cause of a problem and keep writing it down instead of fixing it" [14]. Its remedy landed by 4 October as Scope and Authority, lines that code adds to the prompt of every stage that builds or judges: "When this task fixes something, its cause is part of the work, wherever it lives", with refactoring among the engineering choices that are the agent's to make [14]. The authority runs to the cause of *this* task, so friction that is not its cause stays out by design, as the June misfire taught.

**What worked was a session doing the resident's work.** The Drift & Coherence inspector minted three fix tickets between June and August; on 8 October they still sat in Backlog with no comments [8]. On 11 October John started a coherence loop, a session that ran the inspector, waited for his go and set agents on small tickets; all three were done that day, and the next reading found 332 fewer production lines [15]. It was the first of four episodes of one role. From 11 to 26 October an operator session, a long conversation with John setting its frame, did the resident's work: the coherence loop; three rounds of the Theseus method; a run of improvement cycles; and a charter, a standing brief for the session, on the interface [16].

The third Theseus round, on 17 October, shows what that looked like. The session found targets with standing measures, decided which area owns each scattered decision, filed one move per ticket with a written prediction, scored it after merge and measured again [9]. All eight tickets it filed that day merged, each within two and a half hours, every production change replaying against the old code with zero differences in behaviour. On the hardest target, the code where dispatched work enters Harbour, the places holding each shared decision went from 95 to 26. John's cost was "two presses on ruling cards", the buttons by which he answers an agent's question [17]. One day is not a rate, but on that day capability was not what was missing.

The role survived two compactions, when its conversation was summarised to make room, and ended when John turned the frame to other work. Much of what it knew stayed, in committed scripts, a census of decisions held in several places, and a runbook a fresh agent can follow. What went was the standing, a role whose job the place is and that stays when the frame moves. No Drift & Coherence report has landed since 23 October, though it is weekly, because that cadence is a fallback floor, not a trigger [11]. On this record, continuity of method was largely solved, and continuity of standing was not. It leaves a question of shape. This role worked from outside the flow of tickets, as a campaign that can reach every copy of a decision but needs someone to hold its frame. A step inside the flow would reach every ticket, but see only what its ticket touches.

John has an image for this record [3]. His attention is like a hand held out in a film to keep a forcefield in shape. The default pipeline holds a simple shape, one ticket's change; a passage holds a more complex one; John, by hand, holds a more complex one again. Harbour at work is best seen like a film, in motion and zoomed out, not as stills. Every time the hand moved, the shape dissolved. The method stayed; the shape it held was the standing, and that is what went.

## Toward settled

The diagnosis says what is missing. The rest of this essay is about what could hold the shape without a hand held out all the time. The answer arrived as images, from John and from his conversation with Claude, and each is marked as an image where it appears. They are not separate proposals. Each puts one part of a single idea: every task should leave the place a little more settled, and the agent doing the task is the one best placed to say where.

### The ball, and what settled means

John's second picture [3]: a task is a point inside a ball, and the layers around it are the code its diff has to push through to reach the surface. Where the diff catches on those layers, the code it has to push through or work around to land, is where the change *binds*. Tidying first reconciles layers. It shrinks the job and clears out detritus. If every task left the code a little tidier, the way John tries always to leave fewer tickets than he closes, the code would tend toward a settled point. That is the question the steady-base paper asked, "does it only grow?" [6], answered the other way.

Settled is a balance, not smallness: a balance between what the product does and the code it takes to do it. A settled codebase's size tracks what the product does, not the history of how it got there. The charter's retro is the caution [18]. Between 23 and 26 October the charter tidied first, and the moves were clean: byte-identical moves, each checked at zero differences, took the server's `routes/workspace-api.js` from 3,782 lines to 882 and `routes/dashboard.js` from 3,072 to 872. Yet `routes/` as a whole grew from 26,955 lines to 28,559, and the Settings consolidation was copied back out within a day, a new panel repeating `settings.css` line for line. Over the same days the browser's `public/prompt-section.js` grew from 1,479 lines to 2,302 and copied rules the server holds. Read together, and this is the essay's reading rather than the retro's, the tidy reached the server's files while the changes bound in the browser. If that reading is right, tidying where a change does not bind moves stone without settling it, and the question that matters is where this change binds.

### The drill and the chisel

The conversation with Claude on Fable 5.1 read the same record in another image [4]. Agents are drills: all force, pointed by a ticket, powering through friction. A chisel reads friction as information. In Harbour the builder is the one hand in contact with where a change binds, and so, in that conversation's words, "the most valuable thing Harbour generates about its own codebase, where it binds, is thrown away on every run. The mess isn't the waste. The thrown-away signal about the mess is."

Strictly, it is not thrown away. It is written down, as "improvement noticed, not required", 158 times [2], on the tickets of jobs that then closed, with nowhere for it to collect. Scope and Authority made the drill a better drill [14]. Its target is still the ticket.

### The material is the memory

The same conversation's second reading: "A sculptor keeps no notebook of where the stone is" [4]. Code kept legible holds what *is*, and holds it better as it settles. That is what John's shorthand "textbook style" is for [3]; the conversation added that Harbour's code is a fair picture of the opposite, and a timestamp rule in eighteen files bears it out.

This sorts the three things John said the ticket conflates. The code holds what the place is. A small notebook kept with the area holds where it is going and what has been learned about how to move it. The ticket keeps only what was done. The interface lesson was a lesson about how to move in one area. It belonged in the second, and went in the third.

One such notebook already exists, and it needed no new kind of thing. The Costs work [19] is one area being settled in the open: a plan kept with the area (`docs/plans/costs.md`: "Tidy first, in the Theseus moves, then build"), a record ticket that says "Don't dispatch or plan this ticket", and after each tidy a note on what changed, whether the code is better and what is still half-done. On 2 November three tidies gathered five copies of one rule, whether a row's usage is a running total, into `lib/row-usage.js`; gave the places that sum a lineage's usage one fold; and gave one rule to which rows belong to a lineage, which took about $49.50 of double-counted cost off two tickets. Whether such a notebook lives on a ticket or in `docs/plans/` is left open. What matters is that it is kept with the area, not with a job.

### Forecast first: the spine and its offshoot

John starts from a habit [3]. Forecast how hard a task will be, then decide how much to prepare. It feels as test-driven development did when he took it up, writing the test before the code: more work, in the wrong order. "And yet... I've never looked back", because the benefit lands fast, inside the same piece of work. Beck, whose maxim names the half nobody owns, later wrote a book about exactly this choice, *Tidy First?* [20]. The question mark in the title is the forecast: tidy first when the forecast says it pays, and not otherwise.

His shape for where the forecast sits: the spine is the pipeline as it is, and before a task an offshoot asks "why is this difficult?". *Why* names causes: tests that pin today's shape, a decision held in nine places, callers that keep a seam where it is. Causes are facts, and facts can be acted on, where "this file is messy" is a mood. The answer is a menu at different sizes, and each option says what it buys this task:

- just drill;
- a small tidy first;
- consolidate the decision the task touches;
- run the Theseus method on the area.

Drilling stays on the menu, and is often right: narrow, line-cited tickets landed fine even in the most tangled files. "Not essential, as you can always just drill." The offshoot advises. It does not gate.

The Surface Assessment is its ancestor [13]: the same question asked once, at the end of research, as a verdict. The offshoot grows the verdict into a menu, and keeps its rule that what is done must be pulled by this task. The periodicals change job too. In John's shape they stop being inspectors that mint tickets and become the place's film: they track it over time and show where it stands now, so the offshoot can ask whether this task touches a decision the census already knows is held in several places [16].

That is this essay's answer, untested, to the question of shape the diagnosis ended on. The Theseus method works outside the flow, as a campaign: "The method adds no stage to that pipeline" [9], and campaigns end when the frame moves. A step bounded by its ticket, inside, sees one copy of a decision that lives in many. The offshoot sits inside, pulled by a real change and reaching every ticket, and is fed from outside by the film, which sees every copy. When it picks "consolidate", it keeps the method's rule: "Delete in the same change. A helper that callers may adopt later is one more copy" [9]. Drift & Coherence once recommended the opposite for one scattered pattern, "adopt-on-next-touch, no sweep", and adoption stayed flat: the one file written fresh in that window imported the shared helpers and wrote eight raw copies beside them [8]. A change confined to the files it touches can make each one simpler and still not gather a decision that lives in many; seven rebuilds in a row left the eleven tracked decisions in as many files as before, consistent with that but not proof of it [21].

### What it costs, and what it pays

John's case is cost [3]: "A simpler, tidier codebase is simply cheaper by virtue of there being less to do... Spend a little more now, save forever." His comparison is that a task in Harbour takes hours where in a simple project it would take minutes. That is his comparison, not a measured figure, and the record makes the case differently.

What the record prices is size and send-backs. A change that touches a site of a known scattered decision is about five times the size of one that does not, 229 production lines at the median against 45, and its ticket is sent back by review 0.56 times against 0.23, more often within every size band [8]. Size is a price in itself, more to write, review and carry, and a send-back is a paid run: another session reads the ticket and works again. Then there is the later sweep, when the copies a change missed turn up as the escaped defects counted above.

Two things the record does not show. Within a size band, those changes take no more working hours, and they do not escape more often [8]. In the words of the archive's essay on second copies, "The size *is* the cost": a change to a scattered decision is large because it has to be [22]. The measure is partly circular, since touching a scattered decision is part of what makes a change large, and no trend in the cost of a same-sized change has been shown [22].

So the payback depends on traffic. A tidy pays each time its area changes again, and mess nobody touches costs little, so hotspots come first, and "forever" means for as long as the area stays settled. The saving is spread out and counterfactual: a change that was small because its decision had one home leaves no record of the change it would have been. That is why the drill wins on stills and the tidy shows only in motion. The Costs work is building the meter that would let it be seen. Its goal is that every model call Harbour pays for is tracked simply and reliably, each counted once under the issue it was for [19].

### What it would change

**Review.** Review would have to expect "made the change easy, then made the easy change" as the ordinary shape of a pull request. Today review compares each build with its ticket, and a tidy beside the change reads as creep.

**Autopilot's order.** Autopilot, the agent that keeps Harbour busy, follows a fixed order of precedence: a goal a person has set, else a periodical reading `due`, else the top of the stack [11]. A due periodical runs ahead of real tasks; it does not wait for idle time. So a tidying move given the periodical's rank would run ahead of the work, pushed rather than pulled, against the cautions below. John's picture has the film also doing preventive tidying when nothing else is pending, an idle-time chisel. The place in the order that fits pull is under the stack: no goal set, nothing due, nothing waiting. The order names no such rank today. It matters where it goes. John's reason is that a choice which is optional everywhere loses to the drill by default, so the one time that can default to tidying, to taking something away, is time with nothing else in it.

**The prompts.** The pipeline is itself a place nobody lives in. Until October its prompts only grew, and every lesson added sentences. Since 2 October every prompt surface has been frozen at its byte size: a lesson lands as code or a test first, and a raise needs its reason recorded beside the ceiling it raises [7]. In its first week the total did fall, from 568,534 bytes to 342,621, through two real removals: LIN-3300 deleted the meta-prompt and replaced its decision tree with a stage selector, and LIN-3326 shed close-out's and review's authoring bound and pre-merge risk gates [7]. Since 6 October the freeze has held the prompts rather than lightened them: the later fall to 323,966 was ceilings lowered to their measured size, and a new surface on 1 November raised the total to 332,910 [7]. Theseus for the prompts, renewing them piece by piece as the method renews code, is the Fable conversation's reading [4]: the move that would lighten every other, since every session pays for the prompt text in full.

### The cautions

- **Build no new nouns:** no viewer, no notebook as a new kind of ticket, no periodical inspecting notebooks. That is the accretion reflex in a new coat [3]. The Costs notebook shows the work can be done without one.
- **A new step is a new gate, and gates are where prose collects** [6]. The offshoot advises and does not gate, and its own text sits under the same frozen budget as every prompt.
- **Pull, not push.** A step whose job is the place will find something on every task unless it answers to a need: the June misfire again [13].
- **"Textbook style" is a taste, and taste travels by worked examples, not prompt rules** [3]. Another rule would not do it: "ready is the brand" was a rule obeyed exactly while the screen drifted.
- **Do it in one area first.**

## Where the image strains

- **Some contractors have judgement.** The investigation's first check was whether stages lose what was asked: in 611 of the 653 tickets it checked, at least 95% of the request's sentences survive word for word. Two cases the investigation counted as narrowing were good judgement: a builder who caught a self-contradicting spec and said why it deviated, and agents who followed John's steer over a spec line. A contractor who does that is already part resident.
- **Some spirals have nothing to do with tangle.** One ticket went three rounds of plan-review over the reading of a single house rule, on a file that had just been rebuilt.
- **Tidying as the job is not always enough.** Some tickets whose job was to reconcile two parts of the code still added lines, and the charter's clean moves did not shrink the code around them.
- **The investigation repeated the trap it describes,** and so did the first version of this essay, written the same day from its notes. That version's review found it sound; a fresh reader, given it as its audience would be, called it "an investigation report that ends in a reframing" [23].
- **A voyage, not a place.** Another essay in this library, on how to draw the code, argues it should be drawn "as a voyage... and not as a place", because the decision made each week, which plank next, can be seen only in how the code changes [24]. That reads at first as the opposite of this essay's title. John's film reconciles them. The place is real, and it is seen in motion: whether each replacement leaves the ship sounder "can only be judged across replacements, never at one of them" [24].

John's sense of where this leads, in his words: "a marble beacon on the top of a trig point, allowing us to finally leverage Harbour to bring codebases towards settled. Including our own" [3]. A trig point is a surveyor's pillar on a hilltop; this is an image too. It is not a destination. It is the fixed point every change can be surveyed from, and read against: toward settled, or away.
