# Tasks are tasks, and the codebase is a place

Coding agents make good contractors: given a work order, they do the job, usually well, and leave. But a codebase is a place, and nobody lives in it. The agent that notices a loose stair is held to its ticket, so it notes the stair and walks on. The next agent starts fresh and finds the stair again, because what the first one learned was filed under the job, not the place. Every task is looked after. Nobody's job is the place.

This essay comes from a day spent reading the record of Harbour, a system that runs coding agents over a team's tickets. A ticket is a work item in the tracker. Harbour hands each one to a series of stages, each a fresh AI session that reads the ticket and does one job, such as research, planning, plan-review (a second reader that checks the plan before anyone builds), building or review [3]. What follows draws on [the investigation's notes](https://github.com/JKershaw/LinearViewer/blob/bb20e5f7/docs/reviews/_evidence-2026-11-02-ticket-intent/investigation-notes.md), which hold every count, quote and ticket behind it; every figure and quote below is from them unless it carries a marker to another source. It also draws on three earlier papers that studied the code itself, and on the record of what Harbour has tried before; those figures carry markers. The cases are readings, not measurements, and the notes say so too. John Kershaw, the engineer who runs Harbour, gave the investigation its image.

## Nobody's job is the place

A contractor and a resident see the same house differently. The contractor is there for the work order, and doing that well means not wandering off to do other work. The resident walks past the squeaky stair every day and one morning fixes it on the way. Harbour's agents are contractors, and good ones. What the codebase lacks is a resident, and three things follow from that. Together they are the argument.

**The one who feels the friction is held to the ticket.** An agent building a feature meets the code as it is: the rule kept in three places, the override stacked on an override. It notices, and says so. In roughly three-quarters of 256 tickets' comment threads, agents record friction they saw and left alone, in words like "pre-existing" and "out of scope, note only". Their write-ups even have a standing field for it, "improvement noticed, not required", which appears 158 times. Then the agent leaves the friction, because the brief tells it to. One agent put the pattern in two sentences: widening the work "would be the scope creep the brief forbids. Recording as a finding for LIN-2149's owner to ticket, not filing it myself".

That is good contractor behaviour. Scope rules exist for real reasons: an agent off its ticket is harder to review. But the rule puts the one who knows most about the stair, the one standing on it, in the one position from which it cannot be fixed. The note goes to an owner, and the place has none; nobody passes that way except the next contractor, also held to a ticket.

**What is learned is filed under the job.** Each stage starts cold. It is a fresh session that reads the ticket, does its job and ends [3]. So whatever an agent learns about a corner of the code goes on the ticket, and the ticket is about the job. Even the research run just before these notes left its findings only on its ticket. When the job closes, the memory closes with it.

John names this as the one decision he would be willing to reopen: the ticket as the source of truth. It conflates the record of the job, the plans, and the place's memory. A resident keeps the place's memory in the place. A contractor keeps it in the folder for the job.

**Nobody owns the first half.** Kent Beck's advice is to "make the change easy (warning: this may be hard), then make the easy change" [2]. Inside a ticket, Harbour does only the second half. The first half, tidying the place so the next change lands clean, belongs to no stage, because no stage's job is the place. John's own version: "we want X, ah it's messy, let's tidy up first, then it lands clean", where tidying is small, isolated steps that each leave the code simpler.

The record shows the first half happens only when it is the job. Since 1 September, 965 commits have touched the app's code, and it has grown by about 78,900 lines. About 100 of those commits shrink it, and nearly all come from tickets whose job is to retire, remove, consolidate or rebuild something. Tidying happens when someone files it as a work order, and the only ones placed to notice that it needs filing are the agents who saw the friction and were told to leave it.

Put together: the friction is felt by someone who may not fix it, written where nobody who lives there will read it, and the tidying that would remove it waits for a ticket nobody is placed to file. None of this is a fault in any agent. Each did its job. That is the point.

## The house from the other side

That is the house seen from the ticket. Three of the archive's papers look from the other side, the code itself, and tell the same story. Foote and Yoder named the shape in 1997: "PIECEMEAL GROWTH gradually allows elements of the system to sprawl in an uncontrolled fashion" [14].

**It only grows, and fastest at the gates.** Since June the stage prompts have grown five- to sixfold, and the growth collects at the gates, the steps that decide whether work passes. When something went wrong, the fix was usually a sentence: eight of the ten tickets in the close-out step's lineage changed no running code. Almost nothing leaves: of 101 ticket citations written into the prompt text, five ever left it [5]. The house gains notices on the wall.

**What grows is the number of places holding one decision.** The code did not get messier in the obvious way: duplicated text fell from 2.5% of production lines to 1.2% while the code grew four and a half times. What grew is the number of places holding one small decision: a tolerant timestamp parse went from one file to eighteen [6]. That is the loose stair at scale, fixed in one place and loose in seventeen others. The Theseus method, the archive's account of renewing a codebase while it runs, calls these two kinds of mess [15]. Code *tangled inside*, one file with long, branching functions, is easy to see, and a rebuild from a fresh design fixes it. Code *scattered between*, one decision written in several places, looks fine file by file, and only gathering the copies into one owner fixes it. The scattered kind costs: 86 of 190 escaped defects (45%) trace to one decision held in more than one place, most often a sibling left behind or a change that landed in fewer places than it needed. That share is partly a measure of looking; among faults found by use it is 30 of 110 (27%) [6]. A review that files tickets had filed tickets nobody worked, and helpers stayed half-finished. In the paper's words, "Deletion finishes" [6].

**Pointed at the place, agents look after it.** On 17 October one operator session, a long conversation with John, ran the third round of the Theseus method, after two rounds of rebuilds, on the place itself. It found targets with standing measures, decided which area owns each scattered decision, filed one move per ticket with a written prediction, scored each against it after merge, and measured again to pick the next [15]. That day it filed eight tickets: seven to free, untie, consolidate or rebuild one tangle, and one housekeeping fix. All eight merged, each within two and a half hours, every production change replaying against the old code with zero behaviour differences. At the dispatch entrance, the places holding its shared decisions went from 95 to 26. John's cost was "two presses on ruling cards" [7].

Pointed at the place, agents look after it well and cheaply. What is missing is someone with the standing to point, day after day.

## Three moments

**The stray blue and the brand.** Reviewers can be thorough about the ticket and blind to the place. One design ticket asked that "ready is the brand", and was built exactly as written: any row with a guessed next step turned teal. Four days later feedback said "the colour that should say 'this one' says nothing", and the guessed steps came out. Over five plan-reviews, "blue" appears about 120 times in its record as reviewers hunted stray blue across 13 stylesheets; the ready rule appears five times, never questioned. Whether the rule served the screen was the place's question, and nobody was there to ask it.

**The monospace body.** The body text stayed monospace from a June decision until 26 October. About 88 local overrides grew up around it, each one a fix in a single spot, and 90 remain on main. One code comment reads "the body is monospace… without it the copy-first key note… leaked into mono". A resident would have changed the body once.

**The spiral.** Some tickets go round plan-review again and again. Narrow, line-cited tickets landed in about half an hour even in tangled files; broad clean-ups took two plan-reviews or none; broad design asks spiralled: the brand ticket and one other hit five each. Plan-review asks whether a class of things is closed; the thing is scattered through the code, so each round finds another member, the plan swells, the brand ticket's to "40k, 13 sheets, 15 test files", and John is asked for a ruling. The notes hold this as a hunch, and so does this essay: a spiral needs tangled code, a broad ask that cuts across it, and a goal that is a judgement rather than a closed check. If it holds, the tangle is the place's condition showing up as a ticket's cost.

## Harbour has tried before

None of this is new to John: "I knew I'd already tried this... dozens of times" [12]. Most tries left no record. Three did, and each gave the place a voice without a resident.

**Inspectors.** Harbour has sixteen periodicals, standing reviews such as Drift & Coherence and Seams & Tangles, John's first answer to "how do we look after the growing pile of code?" The agent that keeps Harbour busy takes an overdue one before the top of the queue unless a person has set a goal [8]. But they visit from outside any task: they write a report, mint a few tickets into the same queue as every other work order, and leave. An inspector can say the stair is loose. It does not live there.

**A budget.** Passages, Harbour's plans for a stretch of work, reserved a maintenance leg, one strand of the plan: whenever an inspector was due, that leg made room for it, from the same pool as every other leg [9]. It reserved room for the inspectors, whose output is more tickets, not for anyone with standing to decide what to tidy. John's verdict is that it missed the point and never really worked [12]; the record holds no measure of its outcome.

**A question turned to serve the ticket.** In June, research was asked to end with a Surface Assessment: "would a refactor make this land better?" That is close to the resident's question. Within a week it misfired, producing a blocking refactor for a seam that had "no feature values consumed yet". The follow-up called it push versus pull: the refactor worth doing is the one the feature demands. A refactor must now name the line in this task that calls it, a necessity gate, and a third verdict was added, "improvement noticed, not required" [10], the field that appears 158 times above.

**What worked was a session doing the resident's work.** The Drift & Coherence inspector minted three fix tickets between June and August; on 8 October they still sat in Backlog with no comments [6]. On 11 October John started a coherence loop: a session ran the inspector, waited for his go, set agents on small tickets, and ran it again. All three tickets were done that day, and the next reading found the import cycles inside `lib/` gone and 332 fewer production lines [11]. The loop's last log entry is from 19 October, and on 23 October it was parked and handed to a charter, the same role's next frame. That day's edition counted 54 decisions held in more than one place, against 43 four days earlier, though the edition says most of the 12 new ones were born in a wider window [11]. The bursts fixed what they were pointed at, and the rest of the place kept accruing.

The loop was one of four episodes of one role. For sixteen days, 11 to 26 October, an operator session, a long conversation with John setting the frame, did the resident's work: the coherence loop (LIN-3449); Theseus rounds 1 to 3 (15 to 17 October); the improvement cycles (LIN-3613, 18 to 20 October); and the charter's Theseus loop on the interface (LIN-3490, 23 to 26 October) [16]. It did not end when a session did: the charter records its state before two compactions on 25 October and carries on after each. It ended when the frame moved: the charter turned to the interface and John closed it on 26 October.

Much of what it knew stayed: the code-shape kit, a census of decisions held in several places inside Drift & Coherence, and a runbook whose rule is that each cycle "moves one of your own steps into a committed script, a Periodical or Harbour's pipeline, or deletes it" [16]. What went was the standing. No Drift & Coherence report has landed since 23 October, though it is weekly, because that cadence is a fallback floor, not a trigger [8]; LIN-3449 sits in Todo and LIN-3613 in Backlog. Continuity of method was largely solved. Continuity of standing was not.

## Where the image strains

- **Some contractors have judgement.** The investigation looked for a stage that leaks a request's intent and found none: in 611 of 653 tickets, at least 95% of the request's sentences survive word for word. Two cases it counted as narrowing were good judgement: a builder who caught a self-contradicting spec and said why it deviated, and agents who followed John's steer over a spec line. A contractor who does that is already part resident.
- **Some spirals have nothing to do with tangle.** One ticket went three rounds of plan-review over the reading of a single house rule, on a file that had just been rebuilt.
- **Tidying as the job is not always enough.** Some tickets whose job was to reconcile two parts of the code still added lines.
- **The investigation repeated the trap it describes,** and so did this essay's first version. Its review found it sound; a fresh reader, given it as its audience would be, called it "an investigation report that ends in a reframing" [4].
- **The house is not empty.** 965 commits touched it in about two months. What is missing is not traffic but someone whose job is the place's condition.

## What a resident would be

The notes leave open who would look after the place, and so does this essay. They name four candidates.

- **A person**, with standing over the place's condition across tickets rather than within one. Nobody holds it yet.
- **An agent** with a continuity a fresh session lacks [3].
- **A memory kept with the code**, where the friction lives rather than on a closed ticket. Someone still has to act on it.
- **A permission** to change the place outside a ticket. A size rule meant for prompt text bounded a public setup guide, and John's reading was that an agent should have asked him whether to go past it.

John's current hunch is a fifth.

- **A step in the pipeline** that answers to the place and to the person's intent, not to the ticket. It would draw on the inspectors' reports or its own judgement, ask John for a ruling where intent is in question, and count tidying what the task touches as its job. It is a hunch for exploring; nothing in Harbour implements it [12]. It could sit after research, with research's map, or just before the first change, where it reaches every ticket.

Among people this is the camp-site rule, "always leave the code behind in a better state than you found it", and Fowler is wary of anything that causes it friction, "such as strong CodeOwnership" [13]. Harbour's scope rule is that friction, held on purpose.

John pictures the place as fractal: the whole system at the top, a single function at the bottom, the tangles in between. The way through is bubbles popping into larger bubbles, a small tangle resolved freeing the larger one it sat in, so it cannot be done in one step [12]. It is the picture behind his "compact (or consolidated) versus diffuse" [7]. A resident's work would be a sequence, not a sweep.

**Inside or outside the flow.** The Theseus method wraps tickets from outside, as a campaign: "The method adds no stage to that pipeline" [15]. Outside, the measures choose targets anywhere and a consolidation reaches every copy of a decision, but it needs someone to start it and hold the frame, and the record above is of bursts that ended when the frame moved. John's step sits inside the flow: it reaches every ticket and is pulled by a real change, as the necessity gate asks [10], but it is a new gate, and a step bounded by what its ticket touches sees one copy of a decision that lives in many. A middle shape: a step inside, fed by the census from outside, that asks whether this ticket touches a decision held in several places and whether it is worth gathering now. Which shape, if any, is the resident is left open.

**What is different this time.** In John's words, "now we have a much more mature view on what it needs to do" [12]. Most of it is the Theseus method [15]. Its standing measures are committed scripts, and the census of copies runs inside Drift & Coherence [16]. Its two kinds of mess say which move fits. An ownership rule says which area a gathered decision belongs to. A replay, itself checked first, judges each change by running old and new code side by side. Each move is predicted before filing and scored after merge; four tickets' predictions hit 20 to 36 of their lines and missed 2 to 6, mostly on effort. A runbook is written so that a fresh agent can run a cycle [16]. There is also a necessity gate a place-minded step could answer to [10], and the method was seen to work, eight of eight on 17 October [7].

**What could still make it fail.** The record already holds three ways.

- "Tidy what you touch" has been tried as advice. Drift & Coherence's fix for one scattered pattern was "adopt-on-next-touch, no sweep", and adoption stayed flat: the one file written fresh in that window imported the shared helpers and wrote eight raw copies beside them [6]. The Theseus method's rule is the opposite: "Delete in the same change. A helper that callers may adopt later is one more copy" [15]. The reason is mechanical. A decision lives in many places, so a change confined to the files it touches can make each of them simpler and still not gather it. Seven rebuilds in a row left the eleven tracked decisions in as many files as before, consistent with that but no proof of it, since their tickets put decisions out of scope [17].
- A new step is a new gate, and gates are where prose collects [5].
- A step whose job is the place will find something on every task unless it answers to a need: the June misfire again, push not pull [10].

Another rule would not do it: "ready is the brand" was a rule obeyed exactly while the screen drifted. Rules are more work orders. Whether one of the five is the resident, or whether the image gives out first, is left open on purpose.
