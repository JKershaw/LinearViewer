# The Theseus Method

*Renewing a codebase plank by plank: gather what is scattered, then rebuild what was gathered*

Prepared for John Kershaw | October 2026

A codebase that agents have grown quickly goes wrong in two ways at once. Some of its units get tangled inside: long functions, deep branching, a file nobody wants to touch. And some of its decisions get scattered between units: the same rule written in six places across four areas, each copy drifting a little from the others. The Theseus method treats these as different problems and takes them in a fixed order. First it finds the scattered decisions and gathers each one into the single place that owns it, freeing the tests that pinned the old places on the way. Only then does it rebuild the gathered unit from a fresh design. Every step is judged from outside, by replaying the old code and the new on the same thousands of inputs, not by a test suite the change may have edited. Agents do the work through the codebase's ordinary ticket pipeline; a person answers the occasional ruling. Almost none of the parts is new: Parnas, Fowler, Feathers, McKeeman and Beck described most of them long ago. What is new is the price. An agent rebuilds a module in an hour or two, so the scarce thing is no longer engineering time but a person's attention, and the method is built to spend the first freely and the second almost never. On one codebase, over three days, seventeen changes made this way merged: eleven rebuilds, three changes that untied four knots, two consolidations and one change to tests alone. Every change to production code replayed with no regression that matters, twenty-one bugs found on the way were fixed, and the last round, the full sequence, cost its owner two presses of a ruling button. This essay describes the method well enough for an agent to run it on another codebase, says where each part comes from, and is plain about what has not yet been shown.

## 1 A ship that keeps sailing

Plutarch tells of the ship Theseus sailed home from Crete, kept by the Athenians for centuries by replacing each plank as it rotted, until philosophers argued over whether it was still the same ship [PL]. The philosophers' question is about identity. The shipwright's is more useful here: how do you replace a plank while the ship is at sea, and which plank first?

Harbour, the codebase this method was worked out on, asked the shipwright's question first. A paper on 15 October looked for the plank to start with, and found the best candidate replaceable only once its contract was written down: the shape it returned had never been specified, its main provider had no fake to test a replacement against, and its credentials were decided outside it [H-theseus]. The next day the first design-first rebuilds came back clean, and John Kershaw, who owns Harbour, named the approach: "It feels like there's something new here. A Theseus refactor, if you will. An ideal way to rebuild." [N2]

A plank is easy to replace when it is a plank: one piece, with clean edges, joined to its neighbours at known points. It is hard when the thing you want to replace is not one piece but a decision whose pieces are nailed into a dozen other planks. You cannot lift it out. You have to gather it first, and the tests that were nailed to it have to come loose too.

![One decision in three states: diffuse, six copies across four areas, one drifted; consolidated, one authority in the area that owns it, still tangled inside, with callers asking it; rebuilt, the same boundary and callers with the inside replaced by small parts](figures/theseus-method/diffuse-compact-rebuilt.svg)

*Figure 1. The method in one picture. A scattered decision (left) cannot be rebuilt, because there is no one thing to rebuild. Gathering it gives it one place and a boundary (middle). Rebuilding then changes only the inside (right), behind a boundary its callers already use.*

## 2 Two kinds of mess, and why the order matters

The first kind of mess is **tangled inside**. One unit, a file or a few, has grown long functions and deep branching. You can see it in a shape measure: count the decision points (each `if`, loop, `&&`, `case`) in each function, and look at the worst one. A rebuild fixes this kind well. Eleven design-first rebuilds on Harbour cut the worst function in their unit by between 29% and 88%, and the count of functions with fifty or more decision points across the codebase fell from 21 to 9 [H-ladder, H-round, H-consolidate].

The second kind is **scattered between**. One decision (which states count as finished, which marker means a run is waiting on a person, what a refusal looks like) is written in several places, often in several areas, and the copies drift. Nothing about any single file looks wrong. The cost arrives when the decision changes: the change has to find every copy, and the one it misses becomes a fault somewhere nobody was looking. This is the kind that costs. On Harbour, changes that touched a site of a known scattered decision were five times the size of others and sent back by review two and a half times as often, and 45% of escaped defects traced to a decision that had not been reconciled [H-coherence]; older research on copied code found the same, that inconsistent changes to copies are frequent and a large share of them are faults [J09]. `the-second-copy.md` makes that argument at length [H-copy].

A rebuild does not fix the second kind, because a rebuild works on one unit and the decision lives in many. Harbour learned this by measurement. Seven design-first rebuilds in a row made every file they touched simpler, and left every named decision in exactly as many files as before; the count of tangled area pairs went up by one [H-round]. Gathering fixes the second kind, and it also sets up the first: once a decision has one place, that place is a unit with a clean boundary, which is exactly what a rebuild needs. John Kershaw, who owns Harbour, put the order in a sentence when the rebuild results came in: "It's conceptually for me essentially compact (or consolidated) versus diffuse, dispersed or fragmented. The latter gets targeted first, sets up the seams, then invites the rebuild." [H-consolidate]

The one place this was tried end to end, a dispatch entrance spread across three route files, shows the difference (Figure 7, in section 8). A rebuild on its own took its worst function from 122 decision points to 63. Freeing its tests and gathering its 24 shared decisions took it to 29, and rebuilding the gathered authority took it to 13.

## 3 The five moves

The method is five moves, each a change you could review on its own, and each allowed to change one thing.

![The five moves and what each may change: Find and Own change no code, Free the seam changes tests only, Consolidate changes where code lives, Rebuild changes the inside of one unit; behaviour is held constant throughout, checked by the replay, except fixes the change names](figures/theseus-method/five-moves.svg)

*Figure 2. What each move is allowed to change. Production code moves in only two of the five, and behaviour in none, except fixes a change names and lists. A move that would change a row it does not own is either the wrong move or two moves.*

1. **Find** the scattered decisions, with measures that run on a schedule rather than with someone's sense of where it hurts. Rank them by how many places hold them times how often those places change.
2. **Own.** For each target, decide which area it belongs to, by a written rule, before any code moves.
3. **Free the seam.** Find the tests that pin where the code sits or how it is written, and replace each with a test of what the code does, before anything moves.
4. **Consolidate.** Give each decision one authority in the area that owns it. Delete the copies in the same change. Sort every difference between copies into fix, deliberate variation, harmless, or unclear, and treat each kind its own way.
5. **Rebuild** the authority from a fresh design, and check by measurement that it really is new.

Around every move sit the same checks: a prediction written down before the work starts, a fresh reader of the ticket, the codebase's own review, and after merge an independent replay of the old code against the new (section 9). Sections 4 to 8 take the moves one at a time; section 13 turns them into a runbook.

## 4 Find: let the standing measures choose

The method starts from measures that run on a schedule, whatever anyone happens to think of the code that week. Harbour calls them the Periodicals. Three kinds of instrument did the finding, and any codebase can build all three.

- **A coherence review** that keeps a ledger of decisions held in several places and of how many places each one holds. On Harbour it is a recurring review document with a short list of named decisions and a script that counts their files.
- **An import map over an area guide.** Someone writes down, once, which paths belong to which area (pages, routes, providers, a catch-all). A script reads every import at a given commit and counts, for each pair of areas, the imports each way. A pair with imports both ways is a **knot**: the two areas cannot be changed or understood apart. Harbour's version reads git objects rather than the working tree, so it measures exactly the commit named.
- **A census of copies**: a parser-based scan for the same rule written more than once. It looks for structural clones with identifiers renamed, sets of string literals restated, records built the same way, constants repeated, and text formats such as headings that two files must agree on. The research literature on clone detection is long [RC07, B98]; this one uses an off-the-shelf JavaScript parser, takes about three seconds a commit, and is a candidate for Harbour's review rather than part of it yet.

These instruments see different things, which is why there are three. The census found all nine of the scattered decisions the review already tracked (one only partly), plus eight it did not, but it cannot see a decision written as a procedure, where the copies are sequences of steps rather than shapes, so at the dispatch entrance it saw one of the twenty-four shared decisions [H-consolidate]. The knot count sees direction and nothing about copies. Untying knots moved the knot count and not the census; consolidating a marker moved the census and not the knots.

Rank what they find by **places × change**: the number of files holding a decision times the number of commits that touched those files in the last ninety days. A scattered decision costs only when it changes, so a rule copied into eight files that nobody has touched in a year can wait; the same rule in files that changed a hundred and forty times last quarter cannot. This is the hotspot idea from Tornhill's work on change history, applied to decisions instead of files [T15].

Two cautions. The measures are also the judge: if the method works, they improve, and later changes in the touched areas land smaller and cleaner. So nobody may move a measure by relabelling. Editing the area guide to make a knot disappear, or renaming a function so a signature stops matching, changes the number without changing the code. Every Theseus ticket forbids both. And the measures register less than the code shows. The clearest result of the entrance consolidation, the count of places holding its shared decisions falling from 95 to 26, is a hand count no standing measure takes; the census moved by three sites. A measure that misses what the method does is a gap in the measures, and worth fixing there.

## 5 Own: choose the cut by the rule

Before any code moves, someone decides where each target belongs. The rule is one sentence:

> **A decision belongs to the area that would have to change if its rule changed.**

Its ancestors are Parnas's 1972 criterion, that each module hides a design decision likely to change [P72], and, closer still, Robert Martin's Common Closure Principle, that things which change for the same reasons belong together [M97]. Here it becomes a question you can ask of a single function, or of each edge in a knot. It settles most cases quickly, and applying it produced five corollaries.

![A knot between the agent API and the pages area: the pages import one fact the API owns, the API imports an issue-state rule from a page module; cutting by ownership moves the rule to providers and keeps the thin edge; cutting the thin edge would copy the API's rule into the page](figures/theseus-method/knot-and-owner.svg)

*Figure 3. A knot from Harbour, and two ways to cut it. The usual instinct is to cut the thinner side. Here the thin edge was right: a page displaying a lifetime the API owns. The thick edge was wrong, and its rule belonged to neither area. Cutting the thin edge would have traded the knot for a scattered decision.*

- **Choose the cut by ownership, not thinness.** In a knot, the side with fewer imports looks easier to cut. Ask the ownership question of each edge instead; the cheap cut is often the wrong one, and sometimes the owner is a third area (Figure 3).
- **Move the decision to its owner, or invert the dependency.** In one knot still open, a provider applies a limit that belongs to the prompt formatter; the proposed fix is for the provider to return totals and the formatter to slice them. Moving the constants into the provider would untie the knot and leave the decision with the wrong owner, so that cut was rejected.
- **A knot unties only by taking something away**: an import, a function, a direction. Across nine months of Harbour's history, all five knots that disappeared went that way, and 23 of the 24 that appeared came from a single new import [H-theseus].
- **A catch-all area owns nothing.** A file lands in "other" or "utils" because nobody named its owner, not because it has none. Before treating a knot through the catch-all as two real owners, ask which importer's area the file belongs to. One of Harbour's knots was born when a consolidation put its new single place in the catch-all.
- **Where only the map is wrong, leave the code alone.** If an import looks like a knot because the area guide filed a file under the wrong area, the fix is a map edit, made separately and said out loud, never a code move dressed up as a cure.

Check the call before launch, against every place that uses, throws or catches the thing being moved. One proposed untie on Harbour, moving an error class to the provider interface, was dropped at this check: a second area also threw the error and defined its codes, so it had two plausible owners and needed a person's call, not an agent's [H-consolidate].

## 6 Free the seam: the tests are part of it

Michael Feathers defined a seam as "a place where you can alter behavior in your program without editing in that place" [F04]. His seam is a place to substitute behaviour for testing. This method borrows the word for a neighbouring idea: a boundary across which code can move, or be rewritten, without anything outside noticing. Callers are outside. So, it turns out, are some of the tests.

A suite written quickly by agents holds two kinds of test that look like guards and act like glue. A **location pin** checks where code sits: it reads a source file as text and asserts that a string such as `requireGrant('dispatch')` appears in it. A **text pin** checks how code is written: an exact passage, indentation included. Both pass today. Both fail the moment correct code moves or is reworded, with behaviour untouched: the opposite of what Beck lists as a property of a good test, that it should not change its result if the structure of the code changes [BD]. And both can miss the failure that matters, when the text survives but stops applying, as when a new route skips the check. Harbour's dispatch entrance was guarded by 63 location pins in 35 files [H-consolidate]; across the whole suite, 146 test files read production source as text [H-surplus]. An earlier rebuild of the same entrance had kept every route's own checks in place "because the source-text censuses pin them by the route they sit in", and came out 86% verbatim: an edit, held there by its tests [H-round]. For two rounds the method had treated the suite as a fixed oracle. It is not. As John put it, "I think currently the assumption is the tests are all correct (and necessary), which isn't really true." [H-consolidate]

![Two tests of one property, no grant no dispatch: a location pin that fails when the check moves and passes when a new route skips it, against a behaviour test that passes when the check moves and fails when it is deleted or skipped; below, the order of work in five steps](figures/theseus-method/tests-on-the-seam.svg)

*Figure 4. One property guarded two ways, and the order in which one replaces the other. The pin raises a false alarm when correct code moves and stays silent when a new route skips the check. The behaviour test does neither.*

**Find the readers by tracing, not searching.** Run the whole suite with a hook in the test runtime that logs every test-side read of the target files. On the entrance this found 35 reader files; an earlier count by searching had missed 13 of them, and the first trace itself missed one that spelled the path with a doubled slash [H-consolidate].

**Then probe them twice.**
- The **move probe** serves the tests a moved version of the files, the bodies relocated into the module that will become the authority, while imports still load the real code. Every failure is a location pin, because behaviour did not change. On the entrance: 37 failing tests in 11 files. Confirm with one real move on a scratch tree, which can show constraints the text probe hides.
- The **perturbation probe** applies behaviour-preserving rewrites, one at a time, and runs the full suite on each: re-indent everything, blank every comment, rename locals, replace a literal with a variable, build an argument before the call, split the file. Every failure pins how the code is written. Nine rewrites of the gathered authority failed no behaviour test, but did fail seven text or location pins and ten source-scanning rules, and turned up four stale entries. Blanking every comment broke nothing, which meant no test kept the reason comments either [H-consolidate].

"Freed" has degrees. A unit can be free to move and still pinned against rewording, which is why the perturbation probe runs again before a rebuild even after the move probe comes back clean.

**Class each pin before touching it.** *Convert* a pin that fixes text or location and constrains the design. *Loosen* a count that should judge the unit as a whole: assert at least one call of each kind rather than an exact number, keeping exact counts only for allow-lists such as the set of permitted writers. *Keep* registration-level checks, whole-tree absence scans and vocabulary rules that cost a design nothing. Report *stale* or vacuous ones, and fix them only inside a conversion of the same test.

**Replace each one in a fixed order** (Figure 4, below), with no exceptions for security guards: land the replacement while the old pin is unchanged and green; in a scratch copy outside the repository, remove the guard, show the replacement fail, restore it and show it pass, and record both runs; only then, in a later commit, loosen or delete the old pin. If a replacement cannot be shown failing, the old pin stays exactly as it is. The entrance's tests-only change landed four additive commits before fourteen loosening ones, recorded 67 such runs, added 139 behaviour tests, left production code byte-identical, and took the move probe from 37 failures to none [H-consolidate].

Four rules keep the replacements honest:
- **At least as strong.** A replacement must fail on every mutation the old pin's witnesses used. A drafted conversion that would have accepted "at least one" writer where the pin demanded the declared set was caught before launch and made exact.
- **Mixed pins change only in their in-scope part.** One test guarded sites inside and outside the entrance together; replacing it whole would have silently dropped a guard on seven sites outside.
- **Sweeps, not counts, for exhaustiveness.** Enumerate the entry points from the framework's own registry, drive every request through the real interface, fail on any route the sweep cannot classify, and assert how many it enumerated.
- **A replacement that stays green with its guard removed is aimed at nothing.** One of the entrance's new sweeps passed on two routes because its fixtures stopped at a 404 before reaching the guard; scoring found it, and the next change closed it. Every sweep needs a positive control. And build secret-shaped test values at run time: a token-shaped literal in a test trips the repository's secret scan.

**Give the conversion rule to everyone who touches the change.** Two failures showed who needs it. A worker asked to move a function proved the move with tests that read the new file's source, because "it is in the new file" is the obvious evidence that something moved; review replaced them. And three plan reviews in a row rejected a conversion the ticket allowed, guarding a text pin as though it were the property, until the loop ran out and a person ruled [H-consolidate]. One sample each, and the remedy is cheap: the ticket names which pins may be converted, the worker's ticket and the reviewers' brief carry the same rule (a pin may go once a behaviour check of the same property has landed and been shown to fail without the guard), and the witness for a move is the replay and the import map, never a check of where the code now sits.

Freeing a seam can cost more than the move it enables. The entrance's took two and a half hours and one ruling, to make room for a consolidation that took an hour and a half. John's view: "agreed, but that's kind of what I expected. It's also very cheap, from my perspective." [H-consolidate] What makes it safe to change the tests at all is that they are not the judge. The replay of section 9 judges behaviour without trusting the suite, so the suite can be improved underneath it.

## 7 Consolidate: one authority per decision

Consolidation gives each decision one home and deletes the rest. The unit is the decision, not the file: several entrances are fine, several answers are not, and a new helper counts as consolidation only when callers stop making the old decision themselves [H-paths].

**Count by a written rule.** A *site* is one place that writes the decision's rule itself; a call to the shared authority is not a site. The ticket gives the rule, a count per decision before and after, and the total. On the entrance the count went from 95 sites to 26, none left in the route files; one decision had been read as 5 sites in the ticket and 14 in the map until a fresh reader made them agree [H-consolidate]. For a single decision a tested `git grep` of the sites that should remain works as well: the "waiting on a person" ticket predicted 23 hits before and 14 after, the authority plus the ten that should stay plus three comment lines, and that is what merged. Take every starting list as something to verify at the commit the work will branch from; the review's own line numbers for that decision were stale, and two inline checks were missing from every list.

**Delete in the same change.** A helper that callers may adopt later is one more copy. Harbour's history has decisions whose shared helper the callers never adopted; every migration pattern ends by deleting the old path, and that is the step that gets skipped [H-copy]. The authority lands, every caller is routed to it, and the losing copies go, in one change a replay can judge whole. The reasons beside each copy (ticket references, "why" comments) move with it and stay beside the authority. That is why one consolidation predicted to remove ten to twenty-five lines added eight: an eighteen-line comment gathered the reasons [H-consolidate]. Judge a consolidation by sites per decision and the measures, not by lines.

**Give it one home module in the owning area,** and name any new file into an area pattern before it merges. The one knot Harbour's second round tied was a helper extracted "so the computation lives here once" that matched no area and fell into the catch-all [H-round]. Model the authority's imports on an exported copy before launch, and write into the ticket that a new knot is not acceptable: if one appears, move that code back and list it as an open question.

**Keep what an outsider can observe.** A consolidation unifies code, not behaviour. Each entrance keeps the order in which it checks things (a request that fails two checks must fail on the same one first), the order of its reads, every log status, its response bytes with their key order, and the key set of anything it stores. A check that belongs to one lane only stays in that lane, passed in as that lane's step, and the authority refuses to run (fails closed) if it is missing.

**Sort every difference between copies, in order** (Figure 5).

![When two copies answer differently: is one plainly wrong, then fix it, only comments inside a consolidation; is the difference deliberate, then keep it as a named parameter; is it unreachable or harmless, then allow it, bounded and listed; otherwise keep today's answer for each caller and write the question up](figures/theseus-method/when-copies-disagree.svg)

*Figure 5. Sorting differences between copies. The numbers at right are the 43 differences found when the dispatch entrance's copies were gathered. The seven fixes were all stale comments; the seven unclear ones produced eight open questions, which went to a standing ticket.*

- **(a) A fix** when one copy is wrong by its own comment, the docs or a test. Inside a consolidation only comments are fixed; a wrong behaviour becomes (d), so the replay's expected result stays at exactly zero and any difference at all is a regression. A plain rebuild may fix listed behaviour bugs, each proved by patching it alone into the old copy and showing nothing else differs.
- **(b) A named parameter** when the difference is deliberate: one caller needs its own answer for a stated reason. The authority takes a visible knob, such as a lane or a mode, with the reason beside it; never a silent branch on who is calling.
- **(c) Bounded fuzz** when no real input reaches the difference, or it is harmless (message wording with the same status and code, console wording). Listed with its reason. Never for a successful reply's status or body, a write to another system and its arguments, a stored-data operation, permission or middleware order, or a named security boundary. Where unsure, it is not fuzz.
- **(d) Keep today's answer and write it up** when it is unclear. Each caller keeps exactly what it does now, the replay confirms it byte for byte, and the question, its options and a recommendation go onto a standing ticket for a later ruling. The run never stops to wait for a person. A blocking question would spend exactly the attention the method exists to save, and a ticket that told the worker to raise one was caught before launch for that reason.

**Replay per caller.** A consolidation joins code that may have drifted, so the replay covers every caller separately, with mutants planted at each old site and expected to be caught at exactly that caller. Where callers deliberately disagree, a divergence probe records, on production-shaped inputs, which callers say yes and where they differ; the report must be identical before and after. On the "waiting on a person" marker, 3,000 such inputs showed callers disagreeing on up to 693 of them by design, and the report did not change by a byte [H-consolidate].

Behind the four classes is a distinction worth stating on its own: **scattered data consolidates mechanically; scattered rules need a decision first.** On that marker every copy held the same set, `{'blocked'}`. Gathering the set was safe and took thirty-five minutes. What had drifted was what surrounds it: which channel counts as a question, what ends a wait, when a question goes stale. Those are rules, and rules have owners, so the change kept today's behaviour and posted two questions instead of guessing. It is one case, so it is an inference, but it predicts where consolidation stalls: wherever the copies agree on the data and disagree on the rule.

Expect the authority to come out as the most complex file in its area. The entrance's held 268 decision points and its worst function had 29. That is the point: the complexity that was spread across three files is now in one place, where a rebuild can reach it.

## 8 Rebuild: design-first, and measurably new

Once a decision has one home, the home is a unit with a boundary, and its callers already use that boundary. Now it can be rebuilt.

**Design-first is the only kind of rebuild that was both clearly cleaner and safe.** The agent reads today's code, writes down what it must do, designs afresh, and only then builds. Harbour tried the alternatives first, on the same module [H-ladder]. An **informed rewrite**, asked to improve the code it was looking at, kept the old shape: 82% of its lines were the old lines, and it built itself a throwaway old-against-new check rather than a new design. A **clean room**, where the agent never sees the old code and works from the tests and a written description, simplified the code but was a lottery: four attempts on identical inputs all passed every test, and one of them changed what users see on 345 of 1,037 real tickets. This is Spolsky's old warning, that the old code holds knowledge nobody wrote down [S00], watched happening. Design-first keeps that knowledge because the rebuilder reads it, and still gets a new shape because it designs before it types. The records' reading of the difference is worth keeping: what anchors an agent to the old code is the instruction, not the sight of it.

The ticket fixes the order of work:
1. **Requirements**, for every entry point: the checks in order, every read and write with its arguments, the stored data's key set, the reply's layout and key order, failures in order, every log line, what happens when a client hangs up. Taken from the code, the docs and the tests.
2. **Design**: the simplest structure that serves every entry point, each function with one line saying what it owns. Where today's structure really is simplest, say so and why. Decide the structure before opening the old file again; one rebuild admitted doing its design with the old file open, and its first attempt was 79% verbatim.
3. **Conversions** of the pins the ticket names, by the order of section 6.
4. **Build**: replace the file as a whole. Don't copy functions or blocks; lines a kept constraint requires, and reason comments, are exempt. No production line changes before step 3 is done.
5. **Replay** (the worker's own, light) and **measure**.

**Say what the plan must contain, and what the plan reviewer judges.** Six items: the requirements; the design, one line per function and where each check runs; each conversion with its replacement and the scratch mutation that shows it failing; the commit order; how behaviour will be shown unchanged; what is out of scope. The reviewer approves a plan that covers the six and breaks no constraint in the ticket, and leaves helper names, parameter shapes and line-level design to code review. The list was added after a plan hit the review loop's bound and needed a ruling. Of the two tickets that carried it, one plan was approved on its third round and the other still ran out [H-consolidate], so it helps less than hoped, or not reliably.

**Agents drift back toward editing, so measure whether the rebuild is new.** Asked for a rebuild of a large file, an agent will often edit it in place: it is easier, and the tests pass. Three of seven rebuilds in the second round drifted that way under an instruction that said not to [H-round]. The check is the **verbatim share**, the share of the new code's non-trivial lines (trimmed; not blank, not only punctuation, not comments or imports) found word for word in the code it replaces. Normalise trailing semicolons, which were worth six points on their own in one case. Design-first rebuilds landed between 37% and 55%; clean rooms between 13% and 19%; edits above 80%. The definition of done carries the line: *report the verbatim share; above about 60%, explain why in the change, or treat it as an edit and redo the design.* Review enforces it. Two first attempts came back at 79% and 73%, were sent back under that line, and merged at 51% and 46% (Figure 6). One earlier rebuild, run before the line existed and whose ticket allowed no test to be converted, merged at 86%: by this measure, an edit.

![Share of the new lines found verbatim in the old code: clean rooms at 13 and 19 percent, ten merged design-first rebuilds between 37 and 55 percent, two first attempts at 73 and 79 percent sent back and merged at 46 and 51, and two changes merged as edits at 82 and 86 percent, against a Done-when line at about 60 percent](figures/theseus-method/where-rebuilds-land.svg)

*Figure 6. Where rebuilds land. The Done-when line sits in the gap between design-first rebuilds and edits. For a multi-file change, read it per region as well as overall: one sent-back attempt had a new design but carried its route bodies across at 81–90%, and the merged version was 69% in the tables that are the contract, 29% in the lane table and 11% in the pipeline.*

**Measure the shape, and expect to trade lines for small units.** Decision points per function (cyclomatic complexity less one), the worst function, the total, the number of functions over ten, before and after, with one tool and its definitions written down. The count rewards splitting and counts every `case`, so read several measures together. A good rebuild shortens the worst function a great deal and may lengthen the file: the entrance authority went from 106 functions to 163 and from 878 code lines to 937, while its longest function fell from 256 lines to 74 and its worst from 29 decision points to 13 [H-consolidate].

**Keep the reasons, and treat comments as part of the contract.** The ticket says: keep every ticket reference and every reason comment beside the code it governs; drop one only with its code, and list the drops. One rebuild without that line cut its comment lines from 967 to 409 and dropped 17 of 51 ticket references [H-round]. Comments steer rebuilds as well as describe them: a wrong comment led two clean rooms into the same display bug, and a clean room given the tests but not the comments broke headings and lists on 317 real texts [H-ladder].

**Tell the pipeline it is one change.** "This is one PR. Don't split it into child tickets." A pipeline that decomposes work will split a rebuild into parts that each make sense and together take five hours, as one did before the line was added [H-ladder].

Rebuilds also find bugs. Writing requirements from the code and replaying old against new found nine bugs and two missing features in the first round and twelve fixes in the second; in one rung, the single handler that did not follow the shared pattern held both of its bugs [H-ladder, H-round].

![The dispatch entrance's most complex function: 122 decision points before; 63 after a design-first rebuild on its own; 29 after freeing the seam and consolidating; 13 after rebuilding the authority](figures/theseus-method/entrance-steps.svg)

*Figure 7. One target, step by step. The rebuild on its own halved the worst function. Freeing the seam and consolidating halved it again before any rebuild, and rebuilding the gathered authority more than halved it once more. No arm rebuilt the first rebuild's output without consolidating it, so "consolidating first made the difference" is an inference, not a measurement.*

## 9 The judge: replay the old code against the new

Classic refactoring leans on the test suite: build a solid suite, then change the code in small steps that keep it green [F99]. The Theseus method cannot lean on it, for reasons the earlier sections have shown. The suite pins location and text, so it fails on correct moves. It is edited by the change it would be judging, because freeing the seam means changing tests. And a rebuild is a new body of code the old tests were never written against: four clean rooms passed every test while one of them changed what users see on a third of real tickets.

So behaviour is judged by an **independent differential replay**: run the old code and the new on the same inputs and compare everything an outsider can see. The idea is McKeeman's differential testing, which judged compilers by running several on the same programs [M98]. What the method adds is who builds the replay, when, and how it earns trust.

![The independent replay: inputs captured from the suite, generated from a seed, and targeted at the change go to the old and new code, each pinned at a commit under the same frozen clock, timezone, environment and fakes; what an outsider sees is recorded, normalised and compared; the replay is trusted only after old against old gives zero, planted mutants are caught, changed lines are covered, and after merge it runs on two seeds](figures/theseus-method/the-replay.svg)

*Figure 8. The judge. It is built outside the pipeline, by a different agent from the one doing the work, before the work starts; it judges the merged result after.*

- **Inputs, in four tiers.** Every call the existing tests make, captured. At least 3,000 generated inputs per seed, stratified so every entry point and every input form is covered, and a share of them deliberately malformed. Targeted cases for every validation in order and every listed difference. And, where the code handles text people wrote, a sample of real text, rendered the way users see it and reported as counts only.
- **Two pinned copies, one frozen world.** The old code from an export of the base commit and the new from the change, each with its own private copy of every module it loads and a fresh instance per scenario. One clock, frozen or virtual; time zone UTC; a scrubbed environment; seeded random numbers and ids; network, DNS and outbound fetches blocked and counted. Nothing in a replay calls a real service.
- **Every channel an outsider can see.** Status; body, parsed and as bytes; headers; calls to each dependency, in order, with their arguments; stored writes, judged as they would be persisted; issued credentials; log and audit events; console output; what the server does after a client hangs up; for text, the display. Normalise only what the contract allows, and report each normalisation as information, because each is a place the replay is blind.
- **Self-checks before it is trusted.** Old against an independently pinned twin of old: zero differences on two seeds. Planted mutants, each a single exact text change, caught on exactly the targets expected and nowhere else; equivalent mutants silent. Line coverage of the old code at least 95%.
- **After merge, again**: the merge's parent against the merge, on two seeds. Each difference is grouped and named: a listed fix (proved by patching that fix alone into the old copy and showing nothing else differs), listed fuzz, or a regression.

On Harbour the replays ran from about 6,000 to about 70,000 inputs each, a few minutes a seed. For every production change made this way they found no regression that matters, and on the last round they reported zero differences [H-ladder, H-round, H-consolidate].

**Two replays catch more than one.** The worker inside the pipeline runs a lighter replay of its own while it works (about a thousand inputs, one seed, the old side from an export of the base, a guard so that no check can pass by testing nothing), and the full unit suite once before opening the change. In the second round the operator's replay caught two key-order changes the workers' replays missed, and one worker's replay caught a privacy leak on a public page that the operator's generator never exercised [H-round].

**Know the blind spots.** A comparison that ignores key order, or sorts keys, cannot see an order change; one replay sorted the keys of a stored argument and missed exactly that, until an order-aware rerun found it. A generator that only makes numbers will never show a field leaking text, so plant recognisable canary values in every field type. Fakes are modelled on the real dependencies, so a mistake in the model is shared by both sides. Requests run one at a time, so interleavings are untested. A virtual clock cannot show real socket timing. And a replay judges behaviour, not design: a change can replay perfectly and still be an edit, which is what the verbatim share is for.

Planted mutants are the method checking its own checker, the idea mutation testing has used since 1978 to ask whether a suite can see faults at all [DLS78, JH11].

## 10 Around every ticket, and where people come in

Each move runs as one ticket through the codebase's own pipeline: plan, plan review, build, code review, merge. The method adds no stage to that pipeline. It adds work before the ticket is filed and after it merges, and it keeps people to two narrow points (Figure 9).

![Every move runs as one ticket wrapped in checks: before launch, the ticket, a committed prediction, a fresh reader and the replay built; the codebase's own pipeline plans, reviews, builds and merges; after merge, the replay on two seeds, the score against the prediction, and a re-measure that picks the next target; people are touched at two points only, a ruling when a review loop runs out and open questions answered later](figures/theseus-method/one-step-wrapped.svg)

*Figure 9. One move, wrapped. The pipeline in the middle is the codebase's own and unchanged. People are touched at two points.*

**Before launch.**
1. **The ticket**, built from the clause list in section 13.
2. **A prediction**, committed before filing: ranges and probabilities for the run (whether it splits, merges, needs a ruling, how long it takes) and for each measure, with what would falsify it. Revised after the fresh reader, saying what changed. It limits the bias of an operator who also scores the result. Scored line by line afterwards, the predictions were mostly right on the measures (20 to 36 hits against 2 to 6 misses per ticket) and wrong on effort.
3. **A fresh reader**: an agent that did not write the ticket re-reads it and every line it cites, at the base commit. It checks that every claim about the code is true, that no words are put in anyone's mouth, that every step can be run by the worker as written, that baselines are current, that each test conversion is at full strength, that the ownership call holds against every use, throw and catch, that the repository is named, and that the open-question and out-of-scope lists are complete. Findings come by severity, each re-checked before it is applied. On the last round, fresh readers found must-fix problems in four of nine drafts and killed a fifth [H-consolidate]: among them a stale baseline, a missing repository name, a test change that would have weakened a guard without any test failing, and a line that would have stopped the run to wait for a person.
4. **The replay**, built and self-checked.

**After merge.** The replay runs on two seeds. A scorer, again a separate agent, checks the prediction line by line and the change's own claims; scorers caught a change that said no ticket reference was dropped when one was, and a complexity figure of "about 25" where the tool said 48. Then the standing measures are read again, and the next target comes from them.

**People.** The method asks a person for two things. When a review loop in the pipeline reaches its bound (here, three send-backs), the pipeline asks for a ruling; the operator pre-fills the suggested answer and its reason, and a person reads it and presses. And unclear differences, class (d), become questions on a standing ticket, answered whenever someone has time. The person also sets the frame: what success means, what to work on, what not to touch, when to pause. On the last round that came to two presses for eight merges, one 54 minutes after it was raised and one after 4 [H-consolidate]. What costs attention is now known from the records: review-loop rulings on heavy tickets, escalation lines written into tickets, requests for live checks at close-out, rulings that change nothing but hold up the next change, slips such as a build in the wrong repository, and an overloaded runner. Each has a counter in the method, though for the wrong-repository slip it is only a stopgap: the ticket names the repository on its first line.

**Running it.** Count cost in a person's attention first, then wall clock, then load on the machine the agents run on, then agent hours. Rebuilds are heavy for that machine; the rule that held was one heavy run (a rebuild or consolidation) and up to two light ones (an untie, a small fix) at a time. Don't steer a running worker; if the process improves mid-run, redo the step later with the better process. Keep a notebook entry at every step, facts apart from labelled inferences, since the write-up is built from it. And have the write-up fact-checked: the last round's draft had stated five inferences as findings.

## 11 What is old here, and what may be new

Most of the method is borrowed, and it is better for it: each borrowed part has decades of use behind it. The table sets each part beside its ancestors and says where the method departs from them.

| Part of the method | Its ancestors | Where the method departs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Replace the system piece by piece while it runs | Plutarch's ship [PL]; Fowler's Strangler Fig, growing a new system around the edges of the old [F04s]; the reengineering patterns' "migrate systems incrementally" [OORP]; Brooks, who by 1995 had withdrawn "plan to throw one away" in favour of building incrementally [B95] | The strangler works at the edge of a whole system; here the unit is one decision's authority inside one codebase |
| A scattered decision is what costs | Parnas's modules that each hide a decision likely to change [P72]; Fowler and Beck's Shotgun Surgery, "when the changes are all over the place" [F99]; DRY's "single, unambiguous, authoritative representation" [HT99]; inconsistent changes to clones causing faults [J09]; Lehman's law that complexity grows unless work is done to reduce it [L80] | The unit is the decision, not the text: copies written as different procedures count, and a clone detector alone cannot see them [RC07] |
| Find and rank | Clone detection [RC07, B98]; Tornhill's hotspots, complexity crossed with change frequency [T15]; the Acyclic Dependencies Principle [M97] | Ranks decisions by places × change rather than files by complexity × change, and treats a two-way pair of areas, not every cycle, as the target |
| The ownership rule | Parnas [P72]; Martin's Common Closure Principle, that what changes together belongs together [M97]; Brooks's "Parnas was right, and I was wrong" [B95] | A test for an existing decision, applied edge by edge in a knot, with the catch-all owning nothing |
| Free the seam | Feathers's seams and characterization tests [F04]; Beck's "structure-insensitive" tests [BD]; Khorikov's resistance to refactoring [K20] | Feathers's seam is a place to substitute behaviour for testing; this method borrows the word for a boundary code can move across, and the pins it frees read source text, which none of these sources discusses |
| Prepare, then change | Beck: "make the change easy (warning: this may be hard), then make the easy change" [B12]; preparatory refactoring [F15]; the Mikado method [EB14]; *Tidy First?* [B23] | Applied to rebuilds, across areas: consolidate first, rebuild second |
| Consolidate | DRY [HT99]; parallel change and branch by abstraction [PC14, BA07]; Google's large-scale changes [W20]; the reengineering patterns, which tie duplication to misplaced responsibility [OORP] | Deletion in the same change, deliberate variants as named parameters, unclear differences kept and written up, behaviour held exactly |
| Rebuild | Spolsky's warning against rewriting from scratch [S00]; clean-room reimplementation [SS02], as distinct from the Cleanroom process [MDL87]; agents rebuilding libraries from specifications [C024] | Design-first: the rebuilder reads the old code, then designs; and the result is measured for newness |
| The verbatim share | Git's rewrite heuristic, which calls a change a total rewrite only when less than about 40% of the file survives [GIT] | Used as a gate, with the line on the other side: the method sends back a rebuild that keeps more than about 60%, so between 40% and 60% it accepts what Git would call an edit |
| The judge | Differential testing [M98]; Twitter's Diffy, which runs old code against old code to measure noise [D15]; GitHub's Scientist, which judged rewrites against the old path on live traffic [S16]; mutation testing [DLS78, JH11]; recent work judging LLM code translations by differential fuzzing [E24] | Old against old must give exactly zero (Diffy subtracts noise instead), planted mutants check the judge rather than the suite, and the replay, not the suite, is the acceptance test |

The literature also argues back in two places, and the method should listen. Kim and colleagues followed clone genealogies and found that many copies are short-lived and diverge soon after they are made, and that many long-lived ones are not locally refactorable [K05]: an argument against consolidating everything, and for ranking by change and keeping deliberate variants, which the method does. And the name is not unique: "Git of Theseus" is an existing tool that measures how long lines of code survive [GT]. It is a different thing, but a reader searching will find it.

What looks new is narrower than the method, and comes in five pieces.

1. **The price.** Every ancestor above assumes that a rebuild is expensive and risky, so it counsels small steps and warns against rewriting. With agents a design-first rebuild of a module costs an hour or two of machine time, and the scarce resource is a person's attention. The method is the classic advice re-weighted for that: rebuild freely, gather first, judge independently, and ask a person almost nothing. The large published uses of language models on codebases, Google's migrations and Airbnb's test conversion, are mechanical changes applied many times [Z25, A25]; neither is a redesign.
2. **The judge replaces the suite.** Differential judging of model output is established for translations [E24], and industry judged human rewrites this way [S16]. A judge that must itself pass an exactly-zero old-against-old run and catch planted mutants, used as the acceptance test for agent rebuilds in place of the suite, was not found in a search of the literature. Google's and Airbnb's migrations were judged by builds, tests and review [Z25, A25], and a study of model refactorings found about one suggestion in fourteen unsafe [L25], which is the case for a judge the agent does not write.
3. **Counters for agent-specific failures.** The verbatim gate has no precedent as a gate. Tests that read source text or pin file location, written by agents and then defended by agent reviewers, are not documented anywhere the search reached; the nearest work shows model-written test oracles pinning what code does rather than what it should do [KDP24], and agents deleting failing tests to pass [IB25]. Harbour could measure this itself, which makes it a question for a paper, not this essay.
4. **Consolidation as a precondition, with its classes.** The reengineering literature puts duplicate detection inside reengineering [OORP], and preparatory refactoring gives the order [F15]. No source found prescribes one authority per decision, the copies deleted in the same change, variants as named parameters and unclear differences ruled later, as a fixed step before a rebuild.
5. **Design-first against the alternatives.** Published results on agents doing design-first rebuilds are thin and recent, and none compares design-first, clean room and in-place editing head to head. Harbour's clean-room result agrees with the one benchmark of agents rebuilding libraries from specifications, where none could fully reproduce a library [C024]. The comparison in section 8 is, as far as the search found, unreplicated.

## 12 What has been shown, and what has not

The evidence is three rounds on one codebase, Harbour, over three days in October 2026, each written up as a paper with its records [H-ladder, H-round, H-consolidate].

| Round | What ran | What it showed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Rebuilds of one to two files, four ways: informed rewrite, clean room in and out of the pipeline, design-first | Design-first was the only kind both clearly simpler and free of visible regressions; clean rooms simplified but one in four broke what users see; 9 bugs and 2 missing features found |
| 2 | Seven design-first rebuilds, 441 to 2,816 code lines | All merged with no regression that matters; worst function down 40–88% in every one; 12 bugs fixed; but scattered decisions did not move, and three of seven drifted toward editing |
| 3 | The full sequence: find, own, free the seam, consolidate, rebuild; plus three unties | Every change merged and every production change replayed with zero differences; knots 20 → 16; the entrance's worst function 63 → 29 → 13; two presses of a person's time |

What that does not yet show:

- **A rate.** Every figure is one run per target. The entrance is one decision cluster in one codebase, built by one fleet of agents.
- **The payoff.** The reason to gather scattered decisions is that later changes in those areas land smaller and get sent back less. None has had time to show it. A delayed re-measure of the touched areas is the real test, and it has not run.
- **That consolidating first is what made the difference.** No arm rebuilt the earlier rebuild's output without consolidating it, so the entrance comparison in Figure 7 is suggestive, not controlled.
- **Why plan review sometimes runs out.** Two of the three heavy tickets in the last round hit the plan-review bound and needed a ruling. Of the two tickets that listed what the plan must contain, one converged and one did not, so the list alone does not explain it.
- **Independence.** One operator chose the targets, wrote the tickets and the replays, and scored the results, with predictions committed first. An outside team running the method from this essay alone would be a stronger test.
- **Other codebases.** Harbour is JavaScript without a build step, with an area guide written by hand and a suite written mostly by agents. A typed codebase, or one with a human-written suite, may need less of section 6 and more of something else.

## 13 Running it: a runbook for an agent

This section is written for an agent asked to run the method on a codebase it has not seen. It assumes the agent works as an **operator**: it chooses targets, writes tickets, builds the judge and scores, while the codebase's own pipeline (or a person reviewing pull requests) does the work. Harbour's instruments are named in brackets as worked examples; nothing here depends on them.

### Before the first move: what to build

| Instrument | What it does | Build notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Area map** | Assigns every source path to an area by pattern, first match wins | Write it by hand, once. Name every module; keep the catch-all small and treat it as owning nothing. Freeze it for the round: changing a label moves the measures. (`areas.json`) |
| **Import map and knots** | For a commit: imports between areas, the two-way pairs (knots), the groups of areas tangled together | Read committed objects, not the working tree, so commit before measuring. Know which constructs count as edges (re-exports did here). Blind to copies, to calls over the network, to data shared through a database. (`seams.mjs`) |
| **Shape** | Per file and function: code lines, decision points, worst function, functions over ten | Any complexity tool, with its definitions written down. It rewards splitting and counts each `case`. (`shape.mjs`) |
| **Verbatim share** | Share of new non-trivial lines found word for word in the old | A two-commit command; normalise formatting such as trailing semicolons; report per region as well as overall. (`verbatim.mjs`) |
| **Replay kit** | Old against new on the same inputs, every channel compared | The largest build: per-side module isolation, a hermetic process, recording fakes, a harness that sends each input to both sides, a comparison that records key order, coverage, a captured-tests tier, mutant self-checks. Per target, someone writes the generator, the fakes and the edge cases, several hundred lines each. (`experiments/replay-kit/`) |
| **Read trace and move probe** | Which tests read the target as text, and which fail when it moves | A file-read hook in the test runtime, plus a script that builds the moved text. |
| **Perturbation probe** | Which tests fail when the code is reworded | A rewriter with a handful of behaviour-preserving rewrites, and a loop that runs the suite on each. (`4c/probe/perturb.cjs`) |
| **Census of copies** (optional) | Groups of sites that restate one decision | A parser-based clone, set, record and constant detector, ranked by files × churn. Expect about one in four of its top groups to be noise. (`census.mjs`) |

Also set up: a notebook, written at every step with facts kept apart from labelled inferences; a standing ticket per area for open questions; and a fixed size for concurrent runs (one heavy, a couple of light).

### The loop

1. **Measure** at the current main: knots, shape, census, the coherence review's list. Record the commit.
2. **Pick a target** by places × churn. Prefer, in this order: a knot that hangs on one import; a decision whose copies hold the same data; a decision cluster at a busy entrance. Before planning a from-scratch rebuild of a whole block, check it has a contract you can check by running it.
3. **Make the ownership call** (section 5) and have a fresh reader check it against every use, throw and catch.
4. **Choose the move.** If the target's tests pin it (run the trace and both probes), the next move is *free the seam*. If its copies are scattered, *consolidate*. If it is one unit with a clean boundary and a complex inside, *rebuild*. A knot is a small consolidation: move the decision to its owner, byte-identical, or invert the dependency.
5. **Model the change** on an exported copy where you can: make it by hand, rerun the import map, the census and the named tests, and predict every row that will move, including rows outside the target.
6. **Write the ticket** (clauses below), the **prediction**, and the **replay**; self-check the replay; run the **fresh reader**; apply its findings. File the ticket and start it.
7. **Wait without steering.** If a review loop reaches its bound, pre-fill the ruling with a recommendation and its reason, and ask the person to press. Never answer a ruling yourself.
8. **Score after merge**: replay on two seeds, shape, verbatim, measures; the prediction line by line; the change's own claims. File what review missed.
9. **Re-measure** and return to step 2. A consolidation is usually followed by a rebuild of its authority.

### Done-when, by move

| Move | Done when |
| --- | --- |
| Untie a knot | The knot is gone from the import map at the merge; no new pair; the area map unchanged; the moved code byte-identical; the moved function replayed with zero differences |
| Free the seam | Only tests changed; the move probe fails no test; every replacement was shown failing without its guard before its pin was loosened; no guard weakened |
| Consolidate | Sites per decision at the target count by the ticket's rule; losing copies deleted; no new knot; per-caller replay zero differences; every difference classed, (d) written up |
| Rebuild | Worst function and total decision points down; verbatim share under about 60%, or explained; reasons kept; replay zero differences, or only listed fixes each proved alone |

### Ticket clauses

Each of these was added because something went wrong without it.
- **Where**: the repository named on the first line; branch from a freshly fetched main; check the base before starting; compare against that base, not against numbers in the ticket.
- **Shape of the work**: one change, not a tree of child tickets; one part at a time, no parallel forks; targeted tests while iterating, the full unit suite once, end-to-end tests left to CI; no live calls to outside services and no paid model calls.
- **What**: the target, its owner and the reasoning; the counting rule with a count column, or a tested grep of the sites that should remain; "deleting the losing copies is part of done"; the starting list marked "verify it, don't trust it", with its commit.
- **Order of work**: requirements, design, conversions, build, replay, measures; no production line before the design is written; don't copy functions or blocks.
- **Tests**: which pins may be converted, and the conversion rule; the order of work for every security pin; never weaken a guard, with each guard listed by location; change only the in-scope part of a mixed pin; build secret-shaped test values at run time.
- **Differences**: the four classes, the never-fuzz list, "where unsure, it is not fuzz", and (d) kept and written up, never an escalation that blocks the run; open questions carried forward word for word from earlier changes.
- **Behaviour to keep**: each entrance's observable order (first failure, reads, logs, reply bytes and key order, stored key sets).
- **Measures**: commit before measuring; the import map at base and head with the same map; no new pair; no edits to the area map; expected changes outside the target listed; shape per file; verbatim share with its Done-when line.
- **The worker's replay**: old from an export of the base, about a thousand seeded inputs, frozen clock, UTC, named channels, expected result zero, every difference explained by class; kept out of the unit tests.
- **The plan**: the six things it must contain, and what the plan reviewer judges.
- **Reasons**: keep every ticket reference and reason comment beside its code; list any dropped.
- **Scope**: an explicit out-of-scope list.
- **Words**: quote people only verbatim, and say which rules are the ticket's own. Remove the operator's own working rules before filing.

### What goes to a person, and what never does

A person is asked only for: the frame (success, scope, when to pause); a ruling when a review loop reaches its bound, pre-filled; an ownership call when two areas both have a real claim; and, later and at leisure, the open questions on the standing tickets. Never: an unclear difference in the middle of a run; a request to check something live; a question a fresh reader or the code could answer.

### Stop and say so

Stop the move, and record why, if: the replay's self-checks fail; a new knot appears and moving the code back does not remove it; a pin's replacement cannot be shown failing; the area map turns out to be wrong for the target (fix the map as its own decision); or the fresh reader finds the ownership call has two real owners.

### The frame

### Scattered decisions, finding and ranking
