# Papers

Short documents, each carrying what it rests on. A **paper** answers one question with the
evidence; an **essay** argues a position from sources. `standard.md` says how each is written.
`proposals.md` lists the questions waiting for one.

| [never-worked-pile](harbour/never-worked-pile.md) | What is in the never-worked pile, and what do the worked chains look like? | Live work in the wrong place: 34 of 40 filings still true at HEAD and 38 self-contained, but 23 of 40 are the parent ticket's own unfinished scope. Worked chains mostly narrow — 54 of 84 hops. |
| [close-out-claims](harbour/close-out-claims.md) | When a close-out files its own unfinished scope next door, does it say so? | Always — 23 of 23 name the filing in the comment that closes the ticket. Five call it the parent's own scope left undone, 18 call it a separate matter now owned elsewhere, and all 23 parents are Done while the filing is unworked. |
| [root-task-ratio](harbour/root-task-ratio.md) | Does the system run out of tasks, or generate them forever? | Neither: collapsing breakdown trees to one unit, 1,484 tickets are 1,340 units and each causes 0.74 further ones. Three in four cause none, but a unit that reaches Done causes 1.52 — the population number is under one only because most filings are never worked. |
| [cheap-implementer](harbour/cheap-implementer.md) | Can a cheap model implement Harbour tickets to Opus's review standard, and what does it cost? | Yes, on small tickets: thirteen PRs from three cheap models, all approved within one Opus round; the subscription's share fell to one Opus review a ticket once Flash close-outs merged them. On harness code Flash was green and wrong four times in six, caught only by running the code against real rows; research first would have saved every extra round. An Opus driver cost what hand conning cost, $32 for six tickets. |
| [capability-ledger-method](harbour/capability-ledger-method.md) | How should Harbour learn which model can do which task? | Keep one plain-English ledger per model of what it has been seen to do, judged by a fixed Opus reviewer, rewritten from the evidence after every run, with an absent entry routing to Opus. Every monitored run already produces the evidence; the router should read the ledger rather than a table of thin cells. |
| [capability-ledger](harbour/capability-ledger.md) | What has each model been seen to do on Harbour's tasks? | Edition 3. Opus 5 does every step and is the judge; Sonnet 5 implements and plans to the gate's standard; the two DeepSeek Flash models implement small, hour-plus, thin and stepped tickets to Opus approval within one round, close out reviewed tickets once a human has read the ledger, and write research plan-review does not fault, while their plans have gone round twice without clearing; GLM-5.3 does the small shapes at eight times the price and shadows review well; gpt-oss-120b and Llama 8B produced no work. |
| [plain-language](harbour/plain-language.md) | Could Harbour adopt the plain-language standard, ISO 24495-1? | Yes, as a reader rule rather than a readability gate. Comments put the outcome up front (68% within 60 words) and name their stage (17 of 21), but only 4 of 21 say whether the reader is needed, and all 155 carry the operator's name. |
| [ticket-record-and-quality](harbour/ticket-record-and-quality.md) | Could the way tickets are written and updated be simplified without losing output quality? | Yes, most of it. Over 184 Done tickets the size of the record predicts nothing: first-pass review approval is 66%, 68% and 64% across the light, middle and heavy thirds of the trail while cost goes $13 to $79, and lane tickets closed in two comments shipped nothing the pipeline's own review did not catch, against seven genuine later-found misses on 121 gated tickets carrying 7,000 to 14,000 words. Reviews do read the problem statement, acceptance criteria and file anchors; the embedded-then-pruned plan, the tenth of words in which the machinery narrates itself, and probably the plan-review gate (42 of 46 plans sent back, no better outcome), they do not. Version 2 adopts `what-the-reviews-checked.md`'s check: 13 later Bugs named a Done ticket, 6 were the review's own filed residue, 7 are genuine. |
| [what-the-reviews-checked](harbour/what-the-reviews-checked.md) | What did the reviews behind the thirteen later-found misses check, and what would have caught each one? | Six of the thirteen bugs are the review's own output, filed from its ledger. Of the seven real misses none needed another round: two were outside every named class and visible only on the running page, two were inside a class the sweep stopped short of, one was a witness filed instead of run, two were close-out claims nobody reads. Twenty review rounds cost $99.81; every fix is cheaper than one. |
| [review-consumption](harbour/review-consumption.md) | Which of a review's sentences does the close-out actually cite, discharge or act on? | About half — 48% of sentences over ten reviews. The `What CI Did Not Prove` ledger is read almost completely (97%), findings 47%, and the check narration, 45% of the review, only 25%. The fix round consumes nothing: the last review is always an Approve. |
| [jev-decision-model](harbour/jev-decision-model.md) | Is Jev, a model that decides but cannot write, a fit for Harbour? | As a gate, not a model: it returns a typed choice or a probability and never text, so it can do none of Harbour's fourteen writing calls, and it lives on a separate alpha endpoint outside the catalog. On simple-dispatcher's refusal fixtures it got 7 of 7 where the regex gets 5, fixing both documented false positives; on the seven real routing fixtures it hit 4 to GPT-5.4-mini's 5 and shared both of mini's misses, two of them at confidence 0.8. Forty-five calls cost $0.004 at 189 ms median. |
| [developer-adoption-ladder](harbour/developer-adoption-ladder.md) | What is known about how developers adopt AI coding tools, and does it support the rung ladder? | Partly. The population has the ladder's shape — ~90% of developers use AI, 59% use an agent at work, ~22% ever let one run unattended, and standing loops are unmeasured (April 2026) — and Anthropic's auto-approve share rises from 20% of sessions under 50 sessions to over 40% at 750+, the one published within-user climb. But the hard step is not 3→4: agent use went 31% to 59% in eleven months, and the stall is handing over without watching, gated by the cost of verifying rather than by budget. Rung 3, where the ladder puts the majority, is measured by nobody. The strongest disconfirming source, tens of thousands of Microsoft engineers, found adoption spread socially (+216% odds beside adopting peers) and prior IDE-assistant use *lowered* agent retention 12–15%. |
| [what-lowers-the-verification-cost](harbour/what-lowers-the-verification-cost.md) | What lowers the cost of verifying agent work, and does a verified artifact change how much a developer supervises? | Verification cost is real and rising in every population source that measures it; the only candidate with a controlled positive result is provenance (HANSEL, not tests or CI), and smaller diffs has a negative result against it across 845,316 PRs. No published study shows a verified artifact changes supervision — two controlled studies that manipulated a verification signal found behaviour barely moved: 94% missed planted sabotage unaided, and 56% still accepted it even after a monitor correctly flagged it. Harbour covers six of nine candidates (tests/CI ledger, plan-first gate, provenance, review-tooling split, bounded tasks, guardrails/golden paths), leaves diff size untouched on purpose, and has zero permission-mode variance to measure supervision against today. |
| [developer-adoption-ladder-earlier-dates](harbour/developer-adoption-ladder-earlier-dates.md) | Where did the developer population sit on the ladder in September 2024, September 2025 and March 2026? | Lower at each earlier date on the instruments that recur (Stack Overflow's use-or-plan figure ran 61.8% → 84% → 85%+), but rungs 3, 5 and 6 stay unmeasured at all three dates and rung 4 has no same-instrument series anywhere in the span — its only two figures are different instruments 49,009 and 906 respondents apart. The sharper finding is the frontier's own stall: the highest rung any generally available tool offered reached rung 4 on 25 September 2025 and had not moved six months later, at the paper's last cutoff — the strongest available evidence against reading the ladder as moving because the tools move. |
| [where-harbour-joins](harbour/where-harbour-joins.md) | How fast is the frontier rung moving, where does it land in six and twelve months, and is each Harbour surface ahead of, level with, or behind it? | The generally-available ceiling stood at rung 4 for eleven months and 25 days at the September 2026 cutoff and projects to rung 4, bounds 4–5, by March 2027 (confidence medium-high), and rung 4–5 by September 2027 (confidence low; no instrument supports rung 6 by then); the population frontier cannot be given a number, resting entirely on one Stack Overflow instrument (31% → 59%). One Harbour surface is level with the ceiling (the bounded task with an evidence-verified merge), two are ahead (passages, the standing loop), one is behind (the grounded next prompt, at rung 3 — unmeasured at every date), and observation cannot be placed without a ruling from John, since ladder v3 gives it no rung at all. |
| [runner-for-strangers](harbour/runner-for-strangers.md) | Which runner and which credential lane can run a stranger's task to a PR? | Neither path can today, and the machine is not why: every credential a run touches is the operator's — the plan login, the `~/.ssh` and `gh` token copied into each session HOME, the workspace token in a file on the host. The box has the only landed code (PR #199 green, parked on seven on-box ledger items and an SSH ruling since 30 August) but clones by filesystem copy of an operator-placed template, never from a URL; cloud execution has no code at all (LIN-1302–1305 all Todo) but its design names the two replacements — a per-task `git clone <url>@<ref>` and injected per-session secrets. Five of the six questions need identical work on both paths; only isolation truly differs, where the box is one shared Unix user. A cheap-tier run is ~$2–3 in tokens and $0.01–$0.06 in compute, so the path is not a cost decision — but the estimate is one Harbour cannot reproduce: the usage relay is default-off and `MODEL_PRICING` has no cheap-model row. Recommended: the box as host, the account's own OpenRouter key through `opencode` as the lane, LIN-1301's prep-and-secrets seam built on the box. Being wrong about the machine costs $18 and a week; being wrong about the lane is LIN-1345's incident with a stranger's hand on it. |
| [what-a-run-must-show](harbour/what-a-run-must-show.md) | What does a person expect while one task runs, and what must a checking surface show for an informed merge? | No display promises cheaper checking — provenance is the one candidate with a controlled positive result, and no study shows a verified artifact changes supervision. The three candidate run shapes are scored, not chosen; the observation-rung ruling stays open; twelve questions go to the LIN-2947 sketches sitting. |

| [learning-while-the-tools-change](harbour/learning-while-the-tools-change.md) | Astra 6 and Opus 5.5, version 4; typeset as [Harbour Archive #7](https://harbour.cat/archive/7). Engineering experience, experience using AI and the capability of the available tools run on three separate clocks, and better tools can take away the mistakes that used to teach — so the capability a team most needs to grow is calibrated distrust, knowing which output to check and which needs no check, and no snapshot of speed or seniority can see it. No cross-section can separate the clocks, however large; following the same people doing comparable work helps, though it is not enough by itself. Leapfrogging, factory electrification, the typesetter incumbents, Einstellung in chess masters and Barley's CT scanners supply the mechanisms; the Trio study, Anthropic's autonomy telemetry and Microsoft's 2026 agent rollout supply the contemporary edges. Version 4 is unchecked. |
